Producers approaching European markets for the first time often treat compliance as a
final step — get the crop, then find out what the buyer needs. That sequence does not
work for tree nuts, because the requirements that matter are determined years earlier
by decisions in the orchard and in the design of the processing facility.

By the time a consignment exists, most of its compliance status is already fixed.

## Maximum residue levels

The EU sets maximum residue levels for pesticide active substances in food. For
almonds, as for everything else, a consignment exceeding an MRL cannot be sold.

What makes this an upstream issue:

**Approved substances differ between jurisdictions.** A product legally registered
and routinely used in the producing country may have no EU MRL, or a very low
default one. Using it during the season disqualifies the crop, and there is no
processing step that removes it.

**The spray programme is the compliance mechanism.** MRL compliance is achieved by
choosing substances with EU MRLs, observing pre-harvest intervals, and applying at
approved rates. That is a planning decision made before the season, not a testing
decision made after.

**Application accuracy matters.** Over-application, poor calibration or spray drift
between blocks can produce residues above the level the programme intended. Sprayer
calibration is a compliance activity.

**Records are the evidence.** Every application — product, rate, date, block,
operator, weather — recorded contemporaneously. An audit will ask, and reconstruction
after the fact is neither convincing nor reliable.

**Practical requirement:** design the spray programme against the EU MRL list for the
target market before the season starts, and verify that every product in the store has
an EU MRL for almonds.

## Aflatoxin

The requirement that carries the most risk, because it is a food-safety limit rather
than a marketing standard and the EU limits for tree nuts are strict.

Aflatoxins are produced by *Aspergillus* species growing on the nut. Growth requires
moisture and time. Control is therefore entirely about **preventing the conditions**,
because there is no treatment that removes aflatoxin from a contaminated batch —
contaminated product is condemned.

The control points, in order of occurrence:

**Harvest timing.** Nuts left too long on the tree or on the orchard floor,
particularly after rain, are exposed to conditions that favour fungal growth. Hull
split with delayed harvest is a specific risk.

**Time from harvest to drying.** The critical interval. Product waiting in the field,
in trailers, or in a queue at the facility is product at risk. This is why receiving
and drying capacity — not shelling capacity — determines how fast harvest can safely
run.

**Drying to a safe moisture content, and verifying it.** Measured, recorded, and to a
specification, not judged by appearance.

**Storage conditions.** Moisture and temperature controlled, and monitored. Product
correctly dried and then stored where it can reabsorb moisture is back at risk.

**Insect damage control.** Damaged kernels are entry points for fungal infection.

**Sorting.** Removing visibly damaged, discoloured and mouldy kernels reduces the
load. Optical sorting is increasingly the standard. Sorting reduces risk; it does not
substitute for the earlier steps.

**Sampling and testing.** Aflatoxin distribution in a lot is extremely heterogeneous —
contamination may be concentrated in a very small proportion of kernels. This makes
sampling protocol critical: a small or badly drawn sample can easily miss
contamination that is present, or find contamination that is unrepresentative. Follow
the prescribed sampling methodology rather than an informal version of it.

**The design implication:** drying capacity and speed are food-safety infrastructure.
Specifying a facility around shelling throughput because that is the number people
quote, and treating drying as ancillary, builds in a permanent aflatoxin risk.

See [Almond Processing and Export Development](/almond-processing-export/).

## Traceability

EU food law requires that food be traceable through the supply chain. In practice, a
buyer or auditor will expect you to identify, for any consignment, which blocks it
came from, when they were harvested, how the product was processed and when, where it
was stored, and what test results apply to it.

This has to be designed into operations:

- **Lot identity maintained through processing.** The point at which product from
  different blocks is combined is the point at which traceability granularity is
  fixed. Combining early is operationally simpler and traceably coarser — and if a
  problem is found, the recall scope is whatever you combined.
- **Records at every transfer** between field, transport, receiving, drying, storage
  and dispatch.
- **A documented system**, not institutional memory.

The commercial argument for granular traceability is risk containment. Fine lot
separation means a problem affects a small quantity. Coarse separation means it
affects everything it was mixed with.

## Hygiene and facility standards

Buyers will expect a documented food-safety management system based on HACCP
principles, and most European retail buyers will require certification to a recognised
scheme (BRCGS, IFS, FSSC 22000 or similar).

This is a facility design matter as much as a procedural one — layout that separates
clean and dirty processes, surfaces and equipment that can be cleaned, pest control,
water quality, staff hygiene facilities, and allergen management. Retrofitting a
facility that was not designed for it is expensive and sometimes impossible.

## Certification and market access

**GLOBALG.A.P.** or an equivalent farm-level scheme is a common requirement for
European retail. It covers food safety, traceability, worker welfare and environmental
management at the production level.

**GeoGAP** — created by the Georgian Farmers' Association with USAID and Chemonics
support — is a scaled-down, locally calibrated stepping-stone toward international
schemes. It is not a substitute for GLOBALG.A.P. in EU retail, and it is not intended
to be. Its function is to make certification achievable for producers who cannot reach
an international scheme, building the systems and habits that a later upgrade
requires.

I am quoted on this in the Chemonics/USAID technical brief:

> "In general, we think that implementation of GeoGAP standards, and going through
> certification, will give farmers [the] ability to also sell their products at higher
> prices in the retail sector."
>
> <cite>Chemonics / USAID Zrda Activity, <em>A Local Certification System in Georgia</em>, October 2021</cite>

**Organic certification**, where sought, is separate again — its own standard, its own
certifying body, its own conversion period, and legal protection on the term itself.

## Product specification

Beyond compliance, buyers specify the product: variety, calibre range, kernel shape and
colour, moisture content, defect tolerances, packaging and labelling.

Variety and calibre are largely determined at planting. *EastFruit* reported Guara and
Supernova designated for European export at Udabno, with calibre in the 29–30 mm range
— which is a market specification driving a planting decision, made once for
twenty-five years.

This is the right order. Identifying the target market before ordering trees is what
allows the specification to be met; discovering the specification afterwards means
matching whatever was planted to whoever will buy it.

## Documentation for shipment

Per consignment: commercial invoice and packing list; certificate of origin;
phytosanitary certificate where required; analysis certificates including aflatoxin
results with sampling documentation; and traceability records linking the consignment
to production.

The pattern worth noticing across this whole article: the documentation
**records** compliance that was created upstream. It does not create it.

## References and further reading

EU requirements described above — maximum residue levels, aflatoxin limits and
sampling methodology, general food law traceability obligations, and hygiene
requirements — are set out in EU legislation and are amended over time. **Verify
current limits, approved substances and sampling requirements against the official EU
sources for the shipment date rather than relying on any secondary summary, including
this one.** Certification scheme requirements should be taken from the scheme owners
directly.
