Deep ripping has a reputation as an unambiguously good thing to do — a vigorous,
obviously beneficial intervention. It is not neutral. It is an expensive, powerful
operation that reorganises soil at depth, and on the wrong site or in the wrong
conditions it makes things worse permanently.

These are the mistakes I have seen most often, roughly in order of how much they
cost.

## 1. Ripping without confirming a restriction exists

The most common and most expensive. A block is ripped because ripping is what you
do before planting, or because a neighbouring block needed it, or because the
machine is already on site.

If there is no restriction, ripping achieves nothing and reduces the soil's bearing
capacity ahead of harvest traffic.

**The fix:** dig a pit. See [How to Identify a Restrictive Soil
Layer](/articles/identify-restrictive-soil-layer/).

## 2. Ripping when the soil is too wet

Covered at length in [Correct Soil Moisture for Deep
Ripping](/articles/soil-moisture-for-deep-ripping/), and worth repeating because it
turns money spent into damage done. Wet soil deforms plastically instead of
fracturing: the slot closes, no fracture network forms, and the tine polishes a
smeared, low-permeability surface along its whole path.

You have paid to install a new restriction in the shape of your implement.

**The fix:** the plastic-limit hand test at working depth, and stop-work authority
for whoever is watching the soil behind the machine.

## 3. Blanket specification across variable ground

One pit, or one soil map unit, extended across a whole block or a whole development.
Restrictions vary in presence, depth and thickness over short distances. A single
specification applied uniformly means some ground is under-treated, some is
over-treated, and the cost of the second subsidises the failure of the first.

**The fix:** enough pits to characterise the variation, and treatment zones that
follow it.

## 4. Bringing unsuitable subsoil into the root zone

The mistake with the worst consequences and the least visibility at the time.

Working deeper than the restriction, on a site where the material below is strongly
sodic, saline or acidic, mixes that material upward or opens a pathway for salts to
move up into the root zone. On semi-arid sites with saline groundwater, this is a
realistic risk rather than a theoretical one.

Sometimes the restrictive layer is doing useful work: perching water and keeping
salts away from where roots grow. Fracturing it removes the protection.

**The fix:** the pit must record what is *below* the restriction, and where there is
any reason to suspect salinity or sodicity — semi-arid climate, saline groundwater,
salt crusting anywhere on site, sodic parent material — sample and analyse before
specifying any depth.

## 5. Ripping down the slope

A rip line running downhill is a linear zone of loose, highly permeable soil aligned
with water movement. It concentrates flow, accelerates it, and becomes a gully in
the shape of the implement path.

On erodible semi-arid soil this can develop within a single significant storm.

**The fix:** rip across the slope. Where that conflicts with row orientation, the
erosion risk takes precedence. See [Ripping Depth, Spacing and
Direction](/articles/ripping-depth-spacing-direction/).

## 6. Partial penetration

The implement reaches into the restrictive layer but not through it. This is worse
than not ripping: the tine tip compacts and smears material at its working depth,
creating a new restriction inside the old one, and no connection is made to the
permeable material below.

Causes: insufficient drawbar pull, soil too hard, implement not designed for the
depth, or a depth specification set by machine capability rather than by the
profile.

**The fix:** know the layer's base depth from the pit, and confirm the machine can
reach through it. If it cannot, either get a machine that can, or accept that the
site cannot be treated and plan around a shallower rooting volume — which may mean
choosing a different crop or a different site.

## 7. Spacing too wide for the conditions

A spacing set from a specification sheet or previous experience, executed in soil
that is fracturing less than expected. Untreated columns remain between passes, and
tree positions falling on them get the original restriction and none of the benefit.

**The fix:** cross-cut trench at the start of the job, measure the actual fracture
width, adjust spacing to the soil's behaviour on the day.

## 8. Ripping after irrigation installation

The sequencing error. Once mainlines and drip lines are in the ground, ripping means
destroying the system or leaving untreated strips along the tree rows — the worst
possible location for them.

It happens because irrigation and soil preparation are often separate contracts on
separate schedules, and irrigation feels urgent as planting approaches.

**The fix:** whoever manages the programme owns the sequence — assessment, drainage,
ripping, settlement, levelling, irrigation, planting — and it is not reorderable.

## 9. Planting into unsettled ground

Ripped soil is loose and settles: under its own weight, through wetting and drying,
and particularly after the first significant rain. Trees planted before settlement
sink as the ground drops around them, ending up too deep, sometimes with the graft
union buried.

The losses show up years later and get attributed to almost anything else.

**The fix:** allow a wetting cycle and a settlement period, re-level, then plant.
Build it into the establishment timetable rather than treating it as slack.

## 10. Not checking the work

The specification said 80 cm at 1.5 m spacing. Nobody verified that 80 cm at 1.5 m
spacing is what happened, and by the time anything suggests otherwise the trees have
been in the ground for three years.

**The fix:** cross-cut pits during the operation, while the machine is on site and
adjustment is still possible. Check depth achieved, lateral fracture continuity,
absence of smearing, real fracture rather than slotting.

## When deep ripping should not be done at all

Consolidating the cases where the correct decision is not to rip:

**No restriction is present.** Nothing to gain, bearing capacity to lose.

**Soil is too wet, and will not dry within the available window.** Wait for next
season. Ripping wet is worse than not ripping.

**The restriction is beyond machinery reach.** Partial penetration worsens
conditions. Better to plan around the constraint honestly.

**The subsoil is unsuitable.** Sodic, saline or strongly acidic material below a
restriction is a reason to leave the restriction in place. This needs an analysis,
not an impression.

**The layer is performing a protective function.** A horizon perching water above
saline subsoil is protecting the root zone.

**Slope and erodibility make it unsafe** without erosion control established first.

**On a deep sand with no cemented layer.** Limited aggregate structure to fracture,
limited plasticity, and rapid re-consolidation. Loosening is possible; durable
improvement generally is not.

**Where the economics favour something else.** Ripping is among the most expensive
single field operations in establishment. On a marginal site the same capital may
achieve considerably more spent on water infrastructure, drainage or erosion
control. That comparison should be made explicitly rather than assumed away.

<div class="gm-firsthand">

The judgement that took me longest to develop was recognising that the right answer
is sometimes "not this block". There is real pressure — commercial, contractual and
psychological — to treat land you have committed to. A machine on site and a
contractor scheduled generates its own momentum, and "we decided not to" is a harder
report to write than "we completed the programme".

But a block that has been assessed and deliberately left alone is a decision. A
block that has been ripped wet, or ripped without a restriction, or ripped into
saline subsoil, is a liability that cannot be undone.

</div>
