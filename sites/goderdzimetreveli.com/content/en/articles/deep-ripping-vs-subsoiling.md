Ask three people in agriculture what the difference is between subsoiling and deep
ripping and you will get three answers, at least one of which will be "nothing,
it's the same thing with a different name". In casual use that is nearly true. In
planning an orchard it is not, because the two operations answer different
questions, cost very different amounts, and one of them is a single irreversible
opportunity while the other is a recurring maintenance task.

## The distinction that matters

Forget depth for a moment. The useful distinction is about **what caused the
problem you are treating**.

**Subsoiling** treats a restriction created by farming — a compacted layer at or
just below the depth of routine cultivation and traffic. Plough pans, tillage pans,
and traffic compaction from repeated wheelings all sit in this category. Because
the cause is ongoing agricultural activity, the problem re-forms, and the treatment
recurs.

**Deep ripping** treats a deeper restriction that farming did not create — a dense
natural horizon, a cemented or indurated layer, long-term consolidation, or a
geological discontinuity. The cause is not going to stop, but neither is it going
to make the layer re-form once it is fractured. So the treatment is a one-off, and
in perennial cropping it is a one-off that has to happen before planting, because
afterwards the ground is inaccessible for the life of the orchard.

| | Subsoiling | Deep ripping |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause of restriction** | Agricultural — tillage and traffic | Natural, structural or long-term consolidation |
| **Typical depth** | Below cultivation depth, moderate | Substantially deeper |
| **Frequency** | Recurring, within a rotation | Once, before establishment |
| **Power requirement** | Moderate | High to very high |
| **Typical context** | Arable, annual crops | Orchards, vineyards, perennial establishment |
| **Reversibility of the decision** | Can be repeated next year | Effectively permanent |

## Why the power difference is not linear

The draught required to pull a tine through soil does not scale in proportion to
depth. It rises steeply — a tine working at twice the depth needs considerably more
than twice the pull, because it is displacing a larger volume of soil against
greater confining pressure, and because deeper material is usually denser and
drier.

This has three practical consequences.

**Machinery requirements jump rather than climb.** A tractor perfectly adequate for
subsoiling may be entirely incapable of deep ripping in the same field. This is not
a matter of working more slowly; below a certain drawbar pull the implement simply
does not reach depth.

**Traction matters as much as engine power.** Power that cannot be transmitted to
the ground is not useful. Weight, ballast, and tyre or track configuration are what
convert engine output into pull, which is why tracked or heavily ballasted machines
earn their cost in this work.

**Cost per hectare rises sharply with specified depth.** This is why a quote for
ripping is meaningless without a depth and a spacing attached to it. Two quotes at
different depths are not comparable, and the cheaper one is often cheaper because
it specifies less work.

## Deciding which a site needs

The decision is made in a pit, not from a catalogue. Dig, look at the profile face,
and answer in order:

**1. Is there a restriction at all?**
No abrupt density change, no platy or massive structure where the surrounding
material is crumb or blocky, no roots turning sideways at a consistent depth — then
there is nothing to treat, and neither operation is indicated. Loosening soil that
is already permeable achieves nothing and reduces its bearing capacity.

**2. At what depth is it, and how thick?**
This is the whole answer to the subsoiling-versus-ripping question. A dense layer at
25–35 cm with looser material above and below is almost certainly cultivation-induced
and is a subsoiling problem. A dense layer starting deeper, or one thick enough that
its base is well below normal working depth, is a ripping problem.

**3. What is below it?**
This question is skipped more often than any other, and it is the one that turns a
good decision into a bad one. If the material beneath the restriction is strongly
sodic, saline or otherwise unsuitable, fracturing the layer can bring that material
into the root zone or allow salts to move upward. On saline or sodic sites,
specifying deep ripping without a salinity assessment is a serious risk. Sometimes
the restriction is doing useful work by keeping something worse away from the roots.

**4. Can available machinery actually reach and fracture it?**
Partial penetration is worse than none. A tine that stops within a dense layer
without fracturing through it can compact and smear the material at its working
depth, creating a new restriction at a new depth.

**5. Will the crop use the volume you would gain?**
For a perennial tree crop with a deep root system in a semi-arid climate, yes,
almost always — additional rooting volume is additional stored water. For a
shallow-rooted annual, the economics are much less obvious.

<div class="gm-firsthand">

The version of this decision I have seen go wrong most often is not choosing the
wrong operation. It is specifying either operation across a whole block on the
basis of one pit, or on the basis of a soil map, when the restriction is present in
part of the block and absent in the rest.

Ripping ground that does not need it is money spent for no return, and it reduces
the bearing capacity of soil that will shortly need to carry harvest machinery.
Blanket specification is easier to plan and to price. It is also how you pay for
treating a problem you did not have.

</div>

## Where the two operations meet

There is genuine middle ground. Some sites have both a cultivation pan and a deeper
restriction, and the sensible answer is a single deeper pass that addresses both,
rather than two operations.

There are also implement configurations designed to work at intermediate depths and
to fracture laterally with winged or angled tines rather than simply slotting.
These blur the categories usefully, and the terminology becomes less important than
specifying what you actually want: **a fracture network of a given lateral extent,
reaching through a layer whose top and bottom depths you have measured.**

That specification is unambiguous whatever anyone calls the operation. It is also
the only form of specification that can be checked afterwards.

## Verifying the work

Whichever operation is done, verify it. Dig a pit **across** the worked line
afterwards and check:

- **Actual depth achieved** against specification — measured, not assumed
- **Lateral fracture** extending toward the adjacent pass, with no untreated column
  between them
- **No smeared or polished surfaces** on the tine path, which indicate the soil was
  too wet and the pass has created a new restriction
- **Fracture, not slotting** — an open slot with unfractured walls means the
  operation has achieved almost nothing

Checking a handful of pits across a block while the machine is still on site is the
difference between having a specification and having a result.
