Almost every expensive mistake in orchard soil preparation traces back to the same
omission: nobody confirmed whether a restriction existed, where it was, or how
thick it was, before deciding what to do about it.

The confirmation is not difficult. It requires an excavator or a spade, an
afternoon, and a willingness to look at what is actually there rather than what the
survey data implies. This article is about how to do that and how to read what you
find.

## Why a soil map is not enough

Soil survey data is genuinely useful. It tells you what soil types occur in an
area, what their typical properties are, and roughly where the boundaries fall. It
is the right tool for deciding where to investigate.

It is not evidence about a particular block, for straightforward reasons: mapping
units are generalisations across areas much larger than a single orchard block;
boundaries are interpolated rather than surveyed metre by metre; and compaction and
consolidation history — which is often what created the restriction — is not a
mapped property at all.

<div class="gm-firsthand">

On the land I have worked, the disagreement between mapped expectation and observed
profile was frequent enough that we changed how we used the survey entirely.
Blocks that mapped as similar behaved differently at depth. Blocks that mapped as
poor were sometimes unrestricted below 60 cm. We ended up treating the map as a
sampling plan — it told us where to dig — rather than as a finding.

That reframing cost time in the first season and saved considerably more
afterwards, because it stopped us specifying uniform treatment across ground that
was not uniform.

</div>

## Digging the pit

**Use an excavator if you have one.** A machine-dug pit is faster, deeper and gives
a cleaner face than hand digging, and depth is the point.

**How deep.** Deeper than you expect the restriction to be, and deeper than you
intend to work. You need to see what is *below* the layer as well as the layer
itself.

**How many.** More than one, and located to test variation rather than to confirm
what you already think. A pit in the best-looking part of the block and a pit in
the worst-looking part tell you more than three pits in similar ground. On variable
land, plan a pit per management unit rather than per block.

**Clean the face.** Cut back the smeared surface the bucket leaves. A machine-cut
face is polished and hides exactly the structural detail you are looking for. Take
a spade or a knife to it until you can see natural structure.

**Safety.** A pit deep enough to be useful is deep enough to be dangerous. Do not
enter an unsupported deep pit. Read the face from outside it, or batter the sides.
This is not a formality — soil pit collapses kill people.

## Reading the profile face

Work down the face and record what changes and at what depth.

### 1. Density change you can feel

Push a knife, a screwdriver or a probe horizontally into the face at intervals down
the profile. You are feeling for the depth at which resistance increases sharply,
and the depth at which it decreases again. Those two numbers — top and bottom of the
restriction — are the most important data you will collect, because they determine
whether the operation needed is subsoiling or deep ripping, and to what depth.

Resistance that rises and does not fall again within reach is a different and more
serious finding: the restriction may be too thick to treat.

### 2. Structure

Look at how the soil breaks. Above and below a restriction you typically see crumb,
granular or blocky structure — aggregates with visible faces, breaking along natural
planes.

In the restriction you see one of:

- **Platy structure** — horizontal plates, characteristic of compaction. Water and
  roots move sideways along the plates rather than down through them.
- **Massive structure** — no visible aggregation, breaking irregularly. The soil
  behaves as one coherent lump.
- **Cemented material** — genuinely hard, not merely dense, and often needing a
  hammer rather than a knife.

The distinction matters for treatment. Platy compaction fractures reasonably
readily when dry. Strongly cemented material may not fracture usefully at all.

### 3. Root evidence — the most direct signal available

If anything has been growing on the site, its roots have already run the experiment
you are trying to design.

Look for roots that **turn horizontally at a consistent depth** and run along a
boundary rather than crossing it. That is unambiguous evidence of a restriction, at
exactly the depth that matters, in this specific soil, under real conditions. No
instrument gives you a cleaner answer.

Also look for:

- Roots **flattened or distorted** where they have forced through a gap
- Roots concentrated in cracks and old channels rather than distributed through the
  matrix — the layer is passable only where something has already made a way
- **No roots at all** below a depth, where the soil above is well rooted

### 4. Colour and mottling

Grey, blue-grey or strongly mottled colours indicate periodic saturation and
therefore poor drainage. Immediately above a restriction, that pattern means water
is perching on the layer — which tells you the layer restricts water as well as
roots, and that drainage may need addressing alongside it.

Orange or rust-coloured mottles and concretions indicate alternating wet and dry
conditions.

### 5. Free water

Standing water in the pit, and the depth at which it appears, is directly
informative. Water entering at a specific horizon indicates a perched water table
on a restriction. Note it, and note the date and recent rainfall.

### 6. What is beneath

The question people forget. Record the material below the restriction, and if there
is any reason to suspect salinity or sodicity — a semi-arid environment, saline
groundwater, salt crusting anywhere on the site, or naturally sodic parent material
— sample it and have it analysed before specifying any deep tillage.

Fracturing a layer that separates roots from saline or sodic subsoil can make the
site worse. This is the single most consequential thing a pit can tell you, and it
is invisible from the surface.

## Penetrometer measurements

A cone penetrometer gives resistance against depth as a number, which makes it
useful for mapping variation across a block once you know from a pit what you are
looking at.

Its limitations need respecting:

- **Readings are strongly moisture-dependent.** The same soil reads very differently
  wet and dry. Comparisons are only meaningful at comparable moisture, which in
  practice means surveying within a short period.
- **Stones give false high readings.** A stone at 40 cm reads like a pan at 40 cm.
- **It tells you resistance, not cause.** It cannot distinguish platy compaction
  from cemented material from a stone layer — and those need different responses.

Used properly the sequence is: pit first to establish what the profile is and what
the restriction consists of; penetrometer afterwards to map how that restriction
varies across the area, at consistent moisture.

Reversing the order produces numbers without meaning.

## Bulk density

Bulk-density sampling — a known volume, dried and weighed — gives the most
quantitative measure of compaction, and is worth doing where a decision is
genuinely marginal or where you want a baseline to measure improvement against
later.

It is slow, needs care to sample the right horizon without disturbing it, and
requires interpretation against the soil's texture, since the density at which
roots are restricted differs substantially between a sand and a clay. It is a
confirmation tool rather than a survey tool.

## Recording it properly

Whatever you find is worth recording in a form that will still make sense in three
years, when someone asks why a block was treated the way it was:

- Location — GPS coordinate, not "the pit near the gate"
- Date, and recent rainfall
- Depth of every horizon boundary
- Structure, colour and density for each horizon
- Root observations, especially the depth of lateral deflection
- Free water depth if present
- **A photograph of the clean face with a tape measure in frame**
- Samples taken and analyses requested

The photograph requirement is not optional. A soil profile photograph without a
scale in it cannot support a statement about depth — which is the main thing it
would be needed for.
