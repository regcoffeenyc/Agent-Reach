Almond orchards in semi-arid conditions are entirely irrigation-dependent, which
means the irrigation system does not support the yield ceiling — it *is* the yield
ceiling. A design decision made once, before planting, constrains the orchard for
twenty-five years.

Most of the serious mistakes are made in sizing and in uniformity, not in equipment
selection.

## Size to peak demand, not average

The most consequential design error, and the most common.

Almond water demand is strongly seasonal. It rises through spring, peaks during the
kernel-fill period in mid to late summer, and falls after harvest. The peak is
substantially higher than the annual average.

A system sized to average annual demand will be unable to meet the peak. That
shortfall arrives during kernel fill — the period when water deficit most directly
reduces yield and kernel quality. So the system fails at precisely the moment
failure is most expensive, every year, permanently.

Sizing to peak requires:

- **Peak crop evapotranspiration** for a mature orchard at full canopy — not for the
  young trees being planted now. The system has to serve the orchard it will become.
- **Application efficiency** factored in — the system delivers less to the root zone
  than it discharges.
- **Leaching requirement** added where water quality demands it (below).
- **Realistic operating hours.** A system that can meet peak demand only by running
  24 hours a day has no capacity for maintenance, no recovery from a breakdown, and
  no margin in an exceptional season.

That last point is a recurring trap. On paper the system meets peak demand; in
practice it can only do so at 100% utilisation, so any failure becomes an immediate
crop-loss event.

## Distribution uniformity

The metric that matters most and is monitored least.

A system delivering the correct *average* volume with poor uniformity over-waters
part of the block and under-waters the rest. This is worse than uniformly slightly
inadequate irrigation:

- **The under-watered fraction** loses yield, and accumulates salt because there is
  insufficient water to leach it
- **The over-watered fraction** loses yield through waterlogging and root disease,
  wastes water, and leaches nutrients below the root zone
- **The block becomes unmanageable as a unit** — no single scheduling decision is
  correct for all of it, permanently

Uniformity is a design property that **degrades in service** through emitter
blockage, wear, and pressure variation across topography. And the degradation is
invisible from a vehicle: the trees look similar for a year or two before the
divergence becomes obvious, by which point it is embedded in the canopy.

**What it requires:**

- **Pressure regulation** across elevation change. On sloping ground, unregulated
  systems deliver very different volumes at different heights.
- **Correct lateral lengths** so pressure loss along a line stays within the emitter's
  compensating range.
- **Pressure-compensating emitters** where topography justifies them.
- **Measurement, not assumption.** Periodic flow checks at emitters distributed across
  the block — beginning, middle and end of laterals, top and bottom of slopes.

## Filtration

Blocked emitters are the primary cause of uniformity loss, and filtration is what
prevents them.

Match filtration to the water source: surface water carries organic material and
algae; groundwater carries sand and can carry dissolved iron and manganese that
precipitate in the lines; recycled or stored water can carry biological growth.

Design points:

- Filtration **finer than the emitter's** susceptibility threshold
- **Automatic backflush** on a large system — manual cleaning does not happen reliably
  at scale
- **Pressure differential monitoring** across the filter to indicate fouling
- **Chemical treatment capability** for biological growth and mineral precipitation
- **Flushing valves** at lateral ends, and a flushing regime that is actually carried
  out

Under-specifying filtration is a false economy that shows up as permanent uniformity
loss.

## Scheduling

Irrigate against measured demand and measured soil status, not against a calendar.

**Crop demand** estimated from reference evapotranspiration and a crop coefficient
appropriate to canopy development and growth stage.

**Soil moisture monitored at more than one depth** — typically within the main root
zone and below it. Two depths tell you something one cannot: whether you are
refilling the root zone or pushing water past it.

**Growth stage sensitivity.** Almonds are most sensitive to water deficit during
kernel fill. Some regions practise controlled deficit irrigation at specific
lower-sensitivity stages, which is a legitimate technique but requires confident
monitoring — it is deficit *by design*, and indistinguishable from deficit by
accident if you are not measuring.

**Post-harvest irrigation** matters for the following season's bud development, and
is the irrigation most often neglected because the crop is already off.

## Fertigation

Applying nutrients through the irrigation system is standard practice in drip-irrigated
orchards, and the reasoning is straightforward: with drip irrigation, the active root
zone is concentrated in the wetted volume, so nutrients placed there are available and
nutrients broadcast outside it largely are not.

Requirements: injection equipment with accurate metering; backflow prevention
(non-negotiable, and often a legal requirement); compatibility checking between
fertiliser products and water chemistry to avoid precipitation in the lines; and a
flushing regime after each injection.

The benefit is precision — small, frequent applications matched to demand through the
season, rather than large infrequent applications that leach.

## Leaching requirement

In semi-arid irrigated agriculture, every irrigation adds salt and there is not
enough rainfall to remove it. Unless a deliberate excess is applied to move salts
below the root zone, they accumulate.

Which means the leaching requirement is part of the water budget, calculated from
irrigation water salinity and the crop's salinity tolerance — and almonds are
relatively salt-sensitive, so the requirement is meaningful.

Two things have to be true for leaching to work:

1. **Water is applied in excess of crop demand** by the leaching fraction
2. **Drainage exists to carry the leached water away**

Leaching without drainage does not remove the problem; it moves salt down to a water
table that then rises. This is why drainage assessment precedes irrigation design
rather than following it.

## Interaction with soil preparation

Two hard sequencing rules, both consequences of the same fact — that you cannot get
under an orchard once it exists:

**Deep ripping precedes irrigation installation.** Always. Installing mainlines and
laterals first means either destroying them to rip, or leaving untreated strips along
the tree rows. See [Ripping Depth, Spacing and
Direction](/articles/ripping-depth-spacing-direction/).

**Drainage precedes ripping.** Fracturing soil into a site with an unresolved water
table produces a fissure network that fills with water.

And a design consequence: soil preparation changes the soil's water-holding and
infiltration behaviour, so irrigation design should be based on the soil's
**post-ripping** properties, not on the pre-treatment assessment.

## Young orchard versus mature orchard

The system must serve a mature orchard but will first operate on newly planted trees
with tiny root systems and minimal canopy.

- **Emitter layout for a young tree** may need to differ from the mature layout, or the
  emitters near each tree need to be usable independently
- **Young trees need frequent, small applications** — their root volume holds almost no
  water
- **Applying mature-orchard volumes to young trees** wastes water, leaches nutrients,
  and can waterlog and kill the trees you have just planted
- Design for **staged operation** so the system can be run appropriately at each phase

## Maintenance as a design requirement

A drip system is a maintenance commitment for the orchard's life, and designing
without planning for that produces a system that performs well for three years and
poorly for twenty-two.

Build in: filter backflush and monitoring; lateral flushing valves and a flushing
schedule; emitter flow checking as a routine, recorded activity; pressure monitoring
at fixed points; and leak detection.

<div class="gm-firsthand">

Uniformity monitoring is the maintenance activity that gets dropped first and costs
most. It produces no visible result on the day it is done, unlike fixing a leak, and
it requires walking the block with a measuring container rather than driving past it.

The consequence appears two or three years later as blocks that are permanently
uneven — trees of different sizes in the same planting, needing different management
that they cannot receive because they share an irrigation zone. By then it is a
structural feature of the orchard, not a maintenance backlog.

## References and further reading

Design principles above — peak-demand sizing, distribution uniformity, filtration
matching, leaching requirement calculation and growth-stage water sensitivity — are
consistent with published irrigation engineering and horticultural extension guidance
from major almond-producing regions. Specific crop coefficients, evapotranspiration
figures and salinity thresholds should be taken from current regional data for the
site in question rather than from general sources.
