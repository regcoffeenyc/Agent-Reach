Machinery planning for a large almond operation is usually approached as a
procurement exercise: what do we need, and what does it cost. That framing produces
fleets that are simultaneously over-invested and incapable, because it does not
address the two questions that actually determine outcomes — what the chain's
constraint is, and whether the machinery can do the soil work the site requires.

## The harvest chain and its constraint

Mechanical almond harvest is a sequence:

1. **Shaking** — trunk or canopy shakers remove nuts from the tree
2. **Drying on the orchard floor** (in systems that use floor drying) or direct
   catching
3. **Sweeping** — nuts windrowed from the floor
4. **Pick-up** — the windrow collected
5. **Transport** — to the processing facility
6. **Receiving, hulling and drying** at the facility

**The chain runs at the speed of its slowest element.** This sounds obvious and is
routinely ignored in fleet planning.

If receiving and drying capacity at the facility is the constraint, an additional
shaker adds nothing. It moves nuts off trees faster into a queue that is already
full, and product waiting in the field or in trailers is product losing quality and
accumulating aflatoxin risk.

So fleet sizing is calculated **backward**:

```
processing receiving and drying throughput
   → tonnes per day the facility can absorb
      → transport capacity to deliver that rate
         → pick-up capacity to supply the transport
            → sweeping capacity to supply the pick-up
               → shaking capacity to supply the sweeping
```

Any element sized above the facility's throughput is capital purchased to create a
queue. Any element below it under-uses the facility, which is usually the most
expensive asset in the chain.

## Harvest window length

Capacity requirement is total crop divided by available window. So the window's
length is a fleet-sizing input, and it is determined by:

- **Variety maturity spread.** Multiple varieties maturing at different times spread
  the load. A single variety across a large area concentrates the entire crop into
  one narrow window — which is a capacity problem created at planting, years before
  anyone specifies machinery.
- **Weather risk.** Rain during harvest stops floor operations and raises quality
  risk. The window has to include contingency for days lost.
- **Quality deterioration.** Nuts left too long on the tree or on the ground lose
  quality.

This is why variety selection is a machinery decision as much as a market one. See
[Almond Orchard Development](/almond-orchards/).

## Tractors and soil work

The mechanization requirement that determines what the site can become, rather than
just how efficiently it operates.

**Deep tillage needs drawbar pull, not engine power.** Pull requires weight,
appropriate ballast, and tyre or track configuration adequate to transmit it. Engine
power that cannot reach the ground is not useful. On heavy, dry, compacted soil, if
the available pull cannot bring a ripper through the restrictive layer at the
required depth, that layer stays — permanently, because the ground is inaccessible
after planting.

**Manufacturer figures are optimistic for these conditions.** Draught requirements in
dry, hard, stony ground are substantially higher than in the conditions used for
specification, and progress rates correspondingly lower. Plan on observed rates from
comparable ground, not on brochure figures.

**Weight is a liability outside the job it is required for.** The same mass that
provides traction for ripping causes compaction in every other operation. A fleet
built entirely around the heaviest task compacts the soil it was bought to improve.

The resolution is segregation — by machine, by traffic lane, and by timing:

- Heavy machines reserved for the operations that need them
- Lighter machines for routine spraying, mowing and orchard management
- Traffic confined to permanent lanes, which in an orchard is largely automatic once
  rows are established — making the alignment decisions at establishment more
  consequential than they look
- Avoiding traffic on wet soil, which is an operational discipline rather than an
  investment

See [Agricultural Mechanization](/mechanization/) and [Deep-Ripping and Soil
Preparation](/deep-ripping/).

## Implement matching

The implement does the work.

- **Ripper configuration** — shank number, spacing and geometry matched to the target
  depth and the soil's fracture behaviour, which is observed rather than assumed. See
  [Ripping Depth, Spacing and Direction](/articles/ripping-depth-spacing-direction/).
- **Wear parts as a consumable with a performance effect.** In stony ground, wear is a
  major operating cost — and a worn shank changes the geometry of the work. A blunt
  point smears where a sharp one fractures. Wear affects results, not only budget.
- **Depth control hardware** — consistency matters more than maximum depth.
- **Sprayers** matched to canopy volume and to residue compliance requirements, since
  application accuracy determines whether the crop meets EU maximum residue levels.
- **Mowers and floor management** equipment, since orchard floor condition determines
  harvest efficiency and product cleanliness.

## Orchard floor — a machinery decision made at establishment

Mechanical harvest picks nuts off the ground, so the ground's condition determines
harvest efficiency and product cleanliness.

Requirements: level and smooth enough for sweepers and pick-up machines to operate
close to the surface without picking up soil; free of surface stones, which enter the
product and wear processing equipment; firm enough to carry harvest traffic; and
shaped so water does not pond.

**All of this is decided at establishment** — levelling after ripping and settlement,
stone removal, surface shaping. It cannot be meaningfully improved once trees are in
the ground, and it costs harvest efficiency and product quality every season for
twenty-five years.

## Maintenance capability

On a large operation, maintenance capability is part of the machinery decision, not a
consequence of it.

- **Workshop capacity** proportionate to the fleet, with the skills to do more than
  routine servicing
- **Critical spares held on site** for harvest-window failures. A delay during harvest
  is not recoverable within the season, and a part on a two-week lead time is a
  crop-loss event.
- **Scheduled maintenance timed against the operational calendar** so it happens
  before the window, not during it
- **Operator training.** The same machine in different hands produces measurably
  different work, and in deep tillage the difference is large.
- **Records** — hours, failures, costs — so replacement decisions are made on data

## Serviceability in the actual location

In a market without an established machinery base, this frequently outranks
performance specification.

A machine that cannot be repaired locally within the harvest window is a risk
regardless of how good it is. Assess: parts availability and realistic lead times;
dealer technical support and its response time; whether local technicians have the
skills and diagnostic equipment; and how many similar machines exist in the country,
because a machine with no local population has no informal support network and no
second-hand parts.

<div class="gm-firsthand">

Operator skill is consistently underweighted in mechanization planning, and in deep
tillage it is decisive. The operator controls depth consistency and ground speed, and
makes the judgement about whether conditions are suitable at all — which is the
judgement that determines whether a pass fractures the soil or smears it.

Training operators to read the soil behind the machine, and explicitly giving them
authority to stop when it looked wrong, improved our outcomes more than any
individual equipment decision. It also cost nothing.

</div>
