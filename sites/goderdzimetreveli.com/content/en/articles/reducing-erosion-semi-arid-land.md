On semi-arid agricultural land, erosion is the fastest way to lose the asset itself.
Yield can recover from a bad season; soil that has left the site does not come back.

Two mechanisms operate, and they need different responses. **Water erosion** arrives
in short intense storms falling on ground that infiltrates poorly, and it does most of
its damage in a small number of events. **Wind erosion** works on dry, bare,
fine-textured soil with weak aggregate stability, and it operates continuously
whenever those conditions coincide with wind.

## Layout is the primary control

The most effective erosion control on a perennial planting is decided at
establishment, in the layout, and is nearly impossible to retrofit afterwards.

### Align across the slope, not down it

Rows, traffic lanes and — critically — rip lines should run across the slope rather
than down it.

**A rip line running downhill is a drain.** It is a linear zone of loosened, highly
permeable soil aligned with the direction of water movement. Water concentrates in it,
accelerates, carries soil, and the channel deepens. On erodible semi-arid soil this
can develop within a single significant storm, producing a gully in the exact shape of
the implement path.

Where alignment across the slope conflicts with the preferred row orientation, the
erosion risk takes precedence. Untreated soil columns between ripping passes cost
yield in some tree positions; a gully system costs the soil, and it does not stop
once it has started.

See [Ripping Depth, Spacing and Direction](/articles/ripping-depth-spacing-direction/).

### Design roads as part of the drainage system

Roads and tracks concentrate water by definition — they are compacted, often slightly
sunken, and impermeable relative to their surroundings. A road network designed
without reference to water movement becomes the site's drainage network by default,
and an uncontrolled one.

Design in: cross-fall and cross-drains at intervals; discharge points that release
water onto stable ground or into a designed waterway rather than onto erodible soil;
and alignment that avoids running long distances directly downslope.

### Place windbreaks in the layout

Windbreaks are far more effective when planned as part of block layout than added
later. Retrofitting means finding space between established rows, and losing
production area to trees that take years to become effective.

## Ground cover, and the water trade-off

Cover is the single most effective protection against both mechanisms. In semi-arid
conditions it also competes for the resource that limits the crop, which makes it a
genuine trade-off rather than an obvious good.

The hierarchy, best to worst:

1. **Living permanent cover** in the inter-row — protects the surface, adds organic
   matter, improves aggregate stability and infiltration
2. **Residue or mulch** — protects the surface without transpiring water
3. **Bare soil** — no protection

The competition is real: a living cover crop transpires water the orchard needs, and in
a water-limited system that can directly reduce yield. The decision depends on species
choice, termination timing, the season's rainfall, and how much water is genuinely
available.

Where water does not allow living cover, residue is the fallback and it is
substantially better than nothing. Where neither is possible, the operational
discipline below carries more weight.

## Treat the concentration points

The observation that changes how erosion control is budgeted: **most erosion
originates at a small number of identifiable places.**

Not uniformly across fields — at:

- Road edges and culvert outlets
- Headlands and turning areas
- Block corners, where water from two directions converges
- Natural drainage lines crossing worked ground
- Points where a rip line or wheel track intersects a slope
- Discharge points from any drainage structure

These points are a small fraction of the area and produce a large fraction of the
damage. Treating them — armouring, energy dissipation, stable grassed waterways,
reshaping to spread rather than concentrate flow — is far cheaper and more effective
than any whole-field treatment.

<div class="gm-firsthand">

The practical method that worked was walking the site immediately after the first
significant storm of the season, before anything was repaired, and photographing
every place water had visibly concentrated or moved soil.

That walk identifies the real problem points, which are consistently fewer and more
specific than a desk assessment predicts, and sometimes in places nobody expected.
Fixing them individually over the following dry season addressed most of the site's
erosion at a fraction of what a general treatment programme would have cost.

Repeating the walk each year shows whether the fixes held and where new problems have
appeared as the site changed.

</div>

## Wind erosion

Distinct from water erosion and often underestimated on land that is dry most of the
year.

Conditions that produce it: dry surface soil, fine texture, weak aggregate stability,
no cover, and wind. On a semi-arid site in summer, four of those five are permanent
features.

Controls:

- **Do not cultivate dry, bare soil in windy conditions.** This is an operational rule
  requiring no capital, and it is the single most effective wind-erosion control
  available.
- **Maintain surface roughness.** A rough surface traps moving particles; a fine,
  smooth, well-worked surface is the most erodible condition you can create.
- **Retain residue** wherever possible.
- **Windbreaks** to reduce wind speed at the surface.
- **Avoid excessive secondary cultivation.** Each pass breaks aggregates down further,
  producing exactly the fine, loose material that moves.

The last point deserves emphasis because it runs against a common instinct. A finely
worked, level, tidy-looking seedbed is the most erodible surface achievable. Tidiness
and erosion resistance are opposed.

## Improving the soil's own resistance

Beyond protecting the surface, the soil can be made less erodible.

**Aggregate stability** determines whether aggregates survive raindrop impact and
wetting. It is closely tied to organic matter and to biological activity, is cheap
and quick to test, and is a better indicator of erosion risk than most alternatives.

**Infiltration rate** determines how much rainfall enters rather than running off.
Improving it addresses water erosion at source — water that infiltrates does not
erode. Compaction reduces it, which is one reason compaction management is erosion
management.

**Organic matter** underpins both, and in semi-arid conditions accumulates slowly and
is lost quickly. Gains built over a decade can be lost in one badly timed
cultivation.

**Sodicity** is worth checking specifically. Sodic soils disperse on wetting — clay
particles separate rather than staying aggregated — which makes them highly erodible
and seals the surface against infiltration, producing runoff. If a site erodes more
than its texture and slope suggest it should, sodicity is a likely explanation, and
gypsum amelioration addresses the cause rather than the symptom.

## Monitoring

Erosion is gradual and easy not to notice until it is severe. What makes it visible:

- **Fixed photo points** — same locations, same framing, annually. This is the cheapest
  monitoring available and the most convincing evidence of change in either direction.
- **The post-storm walk**, photographed and recorded.
- **Measuring soil depth at marked points** over years.
- **Recording sediment accumulation** in drains, dams and low points — what
  accumulates there left somewhere else.
- **Watching for exposure of subsoil or stone** at the surface in patches, which
  indicates the topsoil above has gone.
