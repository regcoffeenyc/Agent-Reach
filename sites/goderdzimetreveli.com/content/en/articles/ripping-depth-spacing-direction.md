Once you have established that a restriction exists and that the soil is dry enough
to treat it, three parameters remain: how deep, how far apart, and in which
direction. Each has a plausible-sounding wrong answer.

## Depth

**The rule: deep enough to penetrate and fracture through the restriction, and no
deeper.**

The depth is a property of the soil profile, not of the machine. Your pit gave you
the top and bottom of the restrictive layer; the target is to work through the base
of it and slightly beyond, so that fractures connect the material above the layer
with the permeable material below.

### Why deeper is not better

**Draught rises steeply with depth.** Not proportionally — considerably faster. Each
additional increment of depth costs disproportionately more in fuel, time and wear,
and may exceed the machine's capability entirely.

**Depth beyond the restriction buys nothing.** If the layer bottoms out at 70 cm and
the material below is permeable and root-friendly, working at 110 cm treats soil
that did not need treating.

**It can make things worse.** Working below the restriction may bring up unsuitable
material — sodic, saline or acidic subsoil — into the profile. This is the most
serious reason for restraint, and it is why the pit needs to record what lies below
the layer, not just where the layer is.

**Loosened soil has reduced bearing capacity.** Deeper loosening means a deeper
weakened zone, which then has to carry harvest machinery.

### Consistency beats maximum

Uniform depth across the block matters more than peak depth in the best spots.
Variable depth leaves untreated sections, and trees planted above them perform
differently from their neighbours for the orchard's life — producing a block that
looks patchy and is difficult to manage as a unit.

This makes depth control a real specification item: implement geometry, depth wheels
or skids, operator attention, and ground-speed consistency.

## Spacing

**The rule: close enough that fracture zones from adjacent passes intersect, leaving
no untreated column between them.**

That is a statement about the soil's fracture behaviour, which you have to observe
rather than assume, because it varies with moisture, texture, structure and depth.

### Determining it empirically

The reliable method takes one pit:

1. Make two passes at a trial spacing
2. Excavate a trench **perpendicular** to both passes
3. Look at the cross-section: how far does visible fracturing extend sideways from
   each tine path, and do the two zones meet?

If they meet with overlap, the spacing is adequate or could be widened slightly. If
there is intact, unfractured soil between them, close the spacing.

Doing this at the start of the job, on the actual soil, at the actual moisture,
answers the question definitively. Every alternative is inference.

### What changes the answer

- **Moisture.** Drier, more brittle soil fractures further. See [Correct Soil
  Moisture for Deep Ripping](/articles/soil-moisture-for-deep-ripping/). Spacing and
  moisture cannot be decided independently.
- **Depth.** Fracture is generally narrower at greater depth, under higher confining
  pressure. A spacing that gives continuous fracture at 50 cm may leave untreated
  columns at 90 cm.
- **Texture and structure.** Well-aggregated soils propagate cracks along existing
  planes of weakness. Massive or cemented material fractures less predictably.
- **Tine geometry.** Winged or angled tines fracture wider than plain narrow shanks
  and allow wider spacing at the cost of higher draught.

### The consequence of getting it wrong

An untreated column between passes is not a minor imperfection. If tree positions
happen to fall on those columns — and with a regular planting grid over a regular
ripping pattern, some of them will — those trees have the original restriction
beneath them and none of the benefit that was paid for.

This is why row alignment and ripping pattern have to be planned together rather
than sequentially.

## Direction

Two considerations, which frequently conflict.

### Consideration 1: orchard row alignment

Tree positions should sit in treated ground. The two workable approaches:

**Rip along the future row lines.** Simple, ensures every tree sits on a ripped
line, and economical because you are not treating the inter-row to the same
standard. The limitation is that rooting volume is developed along the row and less
across it.

**Rip in a grid, or at an angle to the rows.** More passes and more cost, but
develops rooting volume in two directions and removes the risk of a tree position
falling between passes.

Which is right depends on how confident you are in the eventual row positions. If
the layout is fixed, along-the-row ripping is efficient. If layout may change — and
on a large development it often does — a grid or angled pattern is insurance, and
the extra cost buys the removal of a permanent risk.

### Consideration 2: slope and water — and this one wins

**A rip line running down a slope is a drain.** It is a linear zone of loosened,
highly permeable soil oriented along the direction of water movement. On erodible
semi-arid soil, that is the beginning of a gully, and it will be a gully in the
shape of your implement path.

Water concentrates in it, moves fast, carries soil, and the channel deepens. What
was intended to improve infiltration becomes the site's primary erosion pathway.

**So on sloping ground, rip across the slope.** On contour, or as close to it as the
machinery can safely and practically work.

Where this conflicts with row orientation, **the erosion risk takes precedence.**
Untreated columns cost yield in some tree positions. A gully system costs the soil
itself, and it does not stop once started.

Where row orientation is genuinely fixed and conflicts with the contour, the answer
is to rip across the slope anyway and manage the row-alignment issue through
spacing — a tighter pattern so that every row position falls within a fracture zone
regardless of orientation.

## Sequencing with drainage and irrigation

Order matters, and one step in particular is not negotiable.

1. **Assessment** — pits, profiles, drainage evaluation, salinity checks
2. **Drainage installation**, if required. Before ripping: fracturing soil into a
   site with an unresolved water table produces a fissure network that fills with
   water and concentrates it with nowhere to go
3. **Deep ripping** — correct moisture, depth, spacing, direction
4. **Settlement period** — a wetting cycle, ideally natural rainfall
5. **Surface levelling and shaping** — after settlement, so the finished surface is
   the settled surface
6. **Irrigation installation**
7. **Planting**

### Step 6 is where the expensive mistake happens

**Deep ripping must precede irrigation installation.** If drip lines, mainlines and
sub-mains are in the ground first, ripping means either destroying them or leaving
untreated strips exactly along the tree rows — which is the worst possible place for
them.

It happens because irrigation installation is often on a different contract and a
different schedule from soil preparation, and because irrigation feels like the
urgent item when planting is approaching. Whoever is managing the programme has to
own this sequence explicitly.

### And step 4 is not slack in the schedule

Ripped ground settles — under its own weight, through wetting and drying, and
notably after the first significant rainfall. Planting into unsettled ground means
trees sink as the soil drops around them, ending up too deep, with stem or graft
union burial. The losses from that appear years later and are attributed to
everything except the planting depth.

Allow a wetting cycle and a settlement period, then re-level, then plant.

## Verifying the work

The specification is worth nothing unless the result is checked. Dig a pit **across**
the rip lines after the operation and confirm:

- **Depth achieved**, measured, against specification
- **Lateral fracture** meeting between adjacent passes, with no intact column
- **No smearing or polishing** on tine paths
- **Genuine fracture rather than slotting** — an open slot with intact walls means
  the pass achieved very little
- **No unsuitable subsoil material** brought into the upper profile

A few pits across a block, while the machine is still on site and adjustments are
still possible, is what turns a plan into an outcome.
