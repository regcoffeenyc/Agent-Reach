If you take one thing from everything written here about deep ripping, take this:
**soil moisture at the time of the operation matters more than depth, spacing,
machine power or operator skill.** Everything else can be adequate and the pass
will still fail if the soil is too wet. And the failure is not neutral — you will
have spent the money and created a new problem.

## Two failure modes

Soil under a moving tine does one of two things, and which one depends almost
entirely on water content.

### Brittle failure — what you want

Dry soil is brittle. Aggregates are held by cohesive forces that, once exceeded,
break rather than yield. When a tine passes through, stress propagates ahead and
outward, and the soil **fractures**: cracks radiate from the tine path, upward and
sideways, well beyond the physical width of the implement.

The result is a network of interconnected fissures. Roots exploit them, water
infiltrates through them, air exchanges through them. The treated volume is much
larger than the volume the tine displaced, which is the whole economic basis of the
operation — you are paying for a fracture network, not for a slot.

### Plastic failure — what you get if you rip wet

Wet soil is plastic. Water lubricates the particle surfaces, aggregates deform
instead of breaking, and the soil **flows** around the tine and closes behind it.

Three things happen, all bad:

1. **No fracture network.** Stress does not propagate; it is absorbed in local
   deformation. You get a slot the width of the tine and nothing beyond it.
2. **The slot closes.** Plastic soil flows back. Within a season the slot may be
   largely undetectable.
3. **The tine polishes the soil it passes through.** The sliding surface smears and
   seals, producing a dense, low-permeability skin along the entire tine path.

That third effect is the serious one. You have created a new restrictive surface, at
depth, in the shape of your implement path — often less permeable than the layer you
were trying to remove. This is why **ripping wet is worse than not ripping at all**.

## Judging the window in the field

The target is soil that is dry, throughout the depth you intend to work. Not dry at
the surface — dry at depth, which is a different question and needs checking at
depth.

### The hand test

Crude, immediate, and reliable enough to make the decision. Take soil from the
target depth — from a pit or a deep auger sample, not from the surface — and:

**Try to roll a thread.** Roll a portion between your palms into a thread a few
millimetres thick.

- **It will not form; it crumbles** → soil is at or below the plastic limit. This is
  the condition you want.
- **It rolls into a coherent thread that bends without breaking** → soil is plastic.
  Do not rip.
- **It rolls easily and feels slick, marks your hands** → far too wet. Do not
  cultivate at all.

**Squeeze a handful.** If it holds together as a cast and glistens, it is too wet.
If it crumbles under light pressure into aggregates, it is workable.

This is the classic plastic-limit field test, and it is the same test used for
deciding whether to travel on soil at all. It takes a minute and it answers the
question.

### Check at depth, and at more than one place

Surface moisture is a poor guide to moisture at 80 cm. The profile can be dust-dry
on top and plastic below, particularly early in the dry season or above a perched
water table. Auger or dig at the working depth.

Do this at several locations across the block. Low-lying areas, shaded areas and
areas above a restriction all dry more slowly, and a block is often ready in one
part and not in another.

### Behind the machine — the check that matters most

The most reliable indicator is the soil's behaviour during the pass. Watch behind
the implement:

- **Good conditions:** visible heave and cracking, soil breaking into clods, a
  fracture pattern extending sideways from the tine line
- **Marginal conditions:** heave without much lateral cracking, larger cohesive
  clods
- **Bad conditions:** a clean slot with smooth walls, soil closing behind the tine,
  a shiny surface on the slot walls

That last picture means stop. Not "carry on more slowly" — stop, and come back when
the soil is drier.

## Why the window is inconveniently narrow

The conditions that make ripping effective are also the conditions that make it
difficult and expensive:

- Dry soil is **hard**, so draught is high and progress is slow
- Wear on shanks and points is **much higher** in dry, hard ground
- The dry window may be short, and **every contractor wants it at the same time**
- In an irrigated system, the dry window may have to be created by withholding
  water — which is only possible before planting

None of that changes the physics. A site that is not dry enough should wait, and
where waiting means deferring planting by a season, that is usually the cheaper
option compared with a failed pass plus a new engineered restriction.

## Interaction with spacing

Moisture and spacing are a single coupled decision, not two independent ones.

Fracture propagates further laterally in drier, more brittle soil. So:

- **Drier soil → wider fracture → wider spacing acceptable**
- **Moister soil → narrower fracture → spacing must be closer to achieve continuity**

Which means a spacing specified for dry conditions, executed in moister conditions,
leaves untreated columns between passes — with the trees very possibly planted into
them. The specification and the conditions have to be reconciled on the day, not in
the office weeks earlier.

The practical answer is to check the achieved fracture width in a cross-cut pit at
the start of the job, and adjust spacing to what the soil is actually doing.

See [Ripping Depth, Spacing and Direction](/articles/ripping-depth-spacing-direction/).

## Timing in the season

In a semi-arid climate with winter-dominant rainfall, the profile is normally driest
in late summer to early autumn. That is the natural window.

Planning implications:

- Programme ripping for the **end of the dry season**, before autumn rain
- Allow time after ripping for a **wetting and settlement cycle** before planting —
  ideally natural rainfall
- Do not compress the sequence to fit a planting date. Ripping into damp soil to
  meet a nursery delivery is a poor trade: the trees arrive on time into soil that
  has not been improved and may have been damaged

Where irrigation exists and the site is pre-planting, withholding water to dry the
profile deliberately is a legitimate tactic — it is the one opportunity to control
the variable rather than wait for it.

## The exception worth knowing

Very sandy soils with little clay behave differently. They have limited plasticity
at any realistic water content and limited cohesion, so they neither fracture in a
sustained network nor smear appreciably. Loosening them is possible; producing a
durable fracture network is not, because there is little aggregate structure to
fracture.

Sandy soils also re-consolidate faster. Whether ripping is worth doing at all on a
deep sand depends on whether the restriction is a genuine cemented or compacted
layer — which does occur in sands — or simply density, which will return.
