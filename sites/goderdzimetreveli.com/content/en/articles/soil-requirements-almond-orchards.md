Almonds are commercially attractive and agronomically demanding. They are also
unusually unforgiving about soil, in two specific ways that between them account for
most orchard failures: they will not tolerate waterlogging, and in a dry climate they
depend heavily on a deep rooting volume to buffer between irrigations.

Both are properties you establish before planting or accept for twenty-five years.

## Depth — the property that sets the ceiling

An almond tree's water supply is the water held in the soil its roots can occupy.
In an irrigated semi-arid system, that stored volume is the buffer between one
irrigation and the next, and the buffer against any interruption in supply.

A shallow rooting volume means:

- **No tolerance for irrigation interruption.** A pump failure or a supply
  restriction during nut fill becomes a stress event within days rather than weeks.
- **Higher irrigation frequency**, and therefore higher operating cost and greater
  system dependence.
- **Reduced total water availability at peak demand**, which caps yield regardless of
  nutrition or canopy management.
- **Greater salinity risk**, because a small soil volume subject to frequent
  irrigation concentrates salts faster.

The practical requirement is a deep, unrestricted profile. Where a restriction
exists within the rooting zone, the decision is whether it can be removed before
planting — see [Deep-Ripping and Soil
Preparation](/deep-ripping/) — because it cannot be removed afterwards.

**How to assess it:** a pit, not a map. Record the depth of any density change,
structural discontinuity or root-limiting horizon, and what lies below it. See [How
to Identify a Restrictive Soil Layer](/articles/identify-restrictive-soil-layer/).

## Drainage — the property that kills trees

Almonds are markedly intolerant of waterlogging. Saturated soil excludes oxygen
from the root zone, and roots that are anaerobic for even short periods are damaged
and become susceptible to root and crown diseases — *Phytophthora* being the
recurring problem in orchards on poorly drained ground.

The awkward part is that this applies on land that is dry most of the year. Semi-arid
sites can have serious drainage problems:

- **Intense storm rainfall on soil that infiltrates poorly**, ponding on the surface
- **Perched water tables** on a restrictive layer, saturating the profile above it
  after irrigation or rain
- **Rising water tables induced by irrigation itself** over a period of years
- **Low-lying positions and depressions** that receive run-on from surrounding ground

**How to assess it:** the pit again. Grey, blue-grey or strongly mottled colours
indicate periodic saturation. Free water in the pit, and the depth at which it
appears, indicates a water table or perching. Orange mottles and concretions
indicate alternating wet and dry conditions.

Assess drainage **before** designing irrigation, and install drainage **before** deep
ripping where both are needed — ripping into a site with an unresolved water table
produces a fissure network that fills and concentrates water with nowhere to go.

## Texture and structure

Almonds do best on medium-textured soils — loams and sandy loams — that combine
adequate water-holding capacity with free drainage and good aeration.

**Sands** drain freely and aerate well but hold little water, requiring frequent
irrigation and offering minimal buffer. Workable with high-frequency drip, but
unforgiving of system failure.

**Heavy clays** hold water well but drain slowly, aerate poorly, and are prone to
both waterlogging and compaction. They are also the soils most likely to have a
restrictive layer and the hardest to treat, because they smear readily when moist.

**Structure matters as much as texture.** A well-aggregated clay loam can perform far
better than its texture suggests, while a structurally degraded loam can behave like
a much heavier soil. What you are looking for in the pit is visible aggregation —
crumb or blocky structure with distinct faces — rather than massive or platy material.

## pH

Almonds prefer a slightly acidic to neutral range, roughly pH 6.0 to 7.5 measured in
water. They tolerate mildly alkaline conditions, which is fortunate because many
semi-arid soils are calcareous and alkaline.

The practical issues at the margins:

- **Above about pH 7.5–8.0**, availability of iron, zinc and manganese declines.
  Lime-induced iron chlorosis is a common and persistent problem on calcareous
  soils, managed through rootstock selection and chelated micronutrient
  application — managed, not solved.
- **Below about pH 5.5**, aluminium and manganese availability rises toward toxicity
  and calcium and magnesium availability falls.

pH is measurable cheaply and should be measured at more than one depth. Surface pH
and subsoil pH frequently differ, and it is the subsoil the roots will occupy.

## Salinity

Almonds are relatively **salt-sensitive** compared with many tree crops, which makes
salinity a first-order consideration on irrigated semi-arid land rather than a
secondary one.

Two sources, and both need assessing:

**Existing soil salinity**, measured as electrical conductivity of a saturation
extract, at multiple depths. Salts frequently accumulate at depth or in specific
positions in the landscape, so a surface sample is not sufficient.

**Salinity introduced by irrigation water.** Every irrigation applies salt. In a
climate without enough rainfall to leach it, that salt accumulates in the root zone
unless a leaching fraction is deliberately applied. Irrigation water perfectly
acceptable in a humid climate — where winter rain flushes the profile annually — can
be unusable in a semi-arid one.

This is the slow failure mode of semi-arid irrigated agriculture: the orchard
performs for years, then declines, and by the time the cause is obvious the salt is
in the profile.

**Practical requirements:** test both soil and water before planting; design a
leaching requirement into the irrigation system where water quality demands it;
ensure drainage exists to carry leached water away, because leaching without drainage
just relocates the problem; and monitor soil salinity over time rather than assuming
the initial assessment holds.

## Sodicity

Distinct from salinity and often overlooked. Sodic soils have a high proportion of
exchangeable sodium relative to calcium and magnesium, which disperses clay
aggregates. Dispersed clay blocks pores, so the soil sets hard when dry, seals when
wet, and infiltrates very poorly.

Sodicity is the reason the pit must record what lies below a restriction. Deep ripping
into sodic subsoil, or mixing it upward, can produce soil physically worse than
before — dispersive, structureless material in the root zone.

Sodic subsoil is common in semi-arid landscapes. It is measurable, it changes the
soil-preparation decision fundamentally, and it is invisible from the surface.

Amelioration with gypsum is possible where sodicity is moderate, but it is a
long-term programme requiring significant material, and it needs to be planned as
part of establishment rather than discovered afterwards.

## Stones

Stones affect almond orchards through the harvest chain more than through tree
performance. Mechanical harvesting picks nuts off the orchard floor; stones on that
floor enter the harvested product, requiring additional cleaning and causing wear in
processing equipment.

Stones also increase wear substantially in deep tillage, and stony ground makes
depth control harder.

Assess stone content at the surface and through the profile, and budget for surface
stone removal as part of establishment if the harvest method requires it.

## An assessment checklist

Before committing to a planting, in this order:

1. **Water** — quantity against peak demand, quality, legal security, reliability in
   the driest year. A "no" here stops the project.
2. **Frost risk at bloom** — almonds flower very early; in a continental climate this
   is the dominant yield risk. Site topography and cold-air drainage matter more than
   mean temperature.
3. **Profile depth and restrictions** — pits, multiple locations, with depths recorded
4. **Drainage** — profile evidence, landscape position, water table
5. **Texture and structure** at each horizon
6. **pH** at multiple depths
7. **Salinity** — soil at multiple depths, and irrigation water
8. **Sodicity** — particularly the material below any restriction
9. **Stones** — surface and profile
10. **Trafficability and slope** for the machinery the crop requires

Items 1 and 2 are the ones most often answered optimistically, and the ones that most
often end projects.
