## The problem

Georgian horticulture has a structural gap between growing and value capture.
Producers grow well and sell unprocessed, which puts them at the thinnest margin
in the chain. The processing value — hulling, shelling, drying, sorting, grading,
and everything beyond — is captured elsewhere, usually outside the country.

For a large orchard this is an existential issue rather than an inefficiency. An
orchard of a few thousand hectares reaching full bearing produces a volume that
cannot be sold unprocessed at an acceptable price. The processing capacity has to
exist by the time the trees bear, or the orchard investment does not return.

## Direction of planning

The counter-intuitive part, and the thing most often got backwards: **processing
capacity determines the harvest calendar, not the reverse.**

Almonds come off in a short window and must be dried quickly to a safe moisture
content. If receiving and drying capacity is below field output, either the field
stops or product waits in conditions where quality degrades and aflatoxin risk
rises. The first is expensive; the second is a food-safety failure.

So the chain is planned backward:

```
processing throughput
   → harvest rate the facility can absorb
      → harvest machinery and transport fleet required
         → area that can be brought to bearing this year
```

Planning it forward — plant the area, then work out how to process the crop — is
how orchards end up selling in-shell at commodity prices.

## Orchard side

*EastFruit* (2021) and *Investor.ge* (2022) report approximately 2,300 hectares of
almond orchard developed from 2017, with more than 675,000 trees of Spanish and
Italian origin. *EastFruit* reports varieties including **Guara, Supernova and
Lauren**, with **Guara and Supernova designated for European export**, and fruit
calibre of **29–30 mm**.

Two decisions there are worth drawing out.

**Self-fertile varieties.** Guara and Supernova are self-fertile. At scale, in a
continental climate with spring frost risk, that removes dependence on pollinator
block layout and on bee activity during a narrow and possibly cold bloom window.
It is a significant de-risking of the crop, decided once, permanently, at planting.

**Variety chosen against a market specification.** Designating specific varieties
for European export means the market requirement — calibre, shape, kernel
characteristics — drove the planting decision rather than being discovered
afterwards. That is the right order, and it only works if the target market is
identified before the trees are ordered.

See [Almond Orchard Development](/almond-orchards/).

## Primary processing

*EastFruit* reported in 2021 that new equipment would allow drying and shelling to
be carried out in-house, rather than the crop leaving the operation unprocessed.

The functions involved: receiving, hulling and shelling, drying, cleaning and
sizing, sorting, and controlled storage.

Of these, **drying** is the one that cannot be compromised. It is the food-safety
step, and drying capacity rather than shelling capacity is frequently the real
constraint on how fast the harvest can run. Storage matters commercially in a
different way: it converts a harvest-time surplus into year-round sales instead of
a forced sale at harvest prices.

## Secondary processing

Secondary processing converts kernel into products with longer shelf life and
different market channels — flour, oil and milk among them.

The economic argument is about the product that fails whole-kernel specification.
Every orchard produces a proportion of broken, undersized or cosmetically imperfect
kernel that is entirely good food but cannot be sold as premium whole kernel.
Without secondary processing, that fraction is sold at a discount. With it, the
discount becomes a raw-material cost for a higher-value product.

This changes the economics of the whole operation and — importantly — reduces the
pressure to hit unrealistic export-grade percentages, which in turn reduces the
temptation to cut corners in sorting.

## Export preparation

*EastFruit* reports export-oriented production with varieties designated for
European markets. A *Caucasus Business Week* interview refers to Europe and Gulf
countries as targets.

EU market access is a systems requirement: maximum residue levels determined by the
orchard spray programme years in advance; aflatoxin control through harvest timing,
rapid drying, storage and sorting; traceability from block to consignment;
HACCP-compliant facility design; and audit-ready documentation.

Almost all of it is decided upstream — in the orchard and in the facility's design.
Very little can be corrected at the point of shipment.

## Regional processing for independent growers

*EastFruit* reported that management intended to offer processing services to other
Georgian almond and walnut growers, with a view to establishing a regional
processing base.

This is the part of the project with the widest effect. A grower with a few
hectares cannot justify hulling, drying and sorting equipment. Without access to it
the crop sells unprocessed at the lowest margin — so quality is not worth
investing in, and the regional crop stays low value in aggregate. That is a trap
that individual effort cannot escape.

Accessible regional processing changes the arithmetic: quality becomes worth
achieving because it becomes sellable. The benefit accrues to growers who have no
relationship with the company beyond using the facility.

## Standards and certification

The Chemonics/USAID Zrda Activity technical brief on GeoGAP (October 2021) quotes
me on the role of certification in market access, and records that Adjara Group
planned to earn GeoGAP certification at its almond orchard, berry farm and
vineyard.

> "Now, when buying GeoGAP-certified products, we have a guarantee that they are
> locally grown, safe (in terms of chemical and biological contamination),
> traceable, and environmentally friendly."
>
> <cite>Chemonics / USAID Zrda Activity, <em>A Local Certification System in Georgia</em>, October 2021</cite>

Documented as a plan. This site does not claim the certification was achieved.

## What was hard

**Matching capacity growth to orchard growth.** Orchard area expands with a
planting decision; processing capacity expands through a capital project with a
long lead time. Keeping them aligned means committing to processing investment
against a bearing area that does not exist yet — which is uncomfortable and
necessary.

**Drying capacity being the real constraint.** It is easy to specify a facility
around shelling throughput, because that is the number people quote. Drying is
what limits how fast harvest can run and is the step where compromise creates a
food-safety problem rather than an efficiency loss.

**Quality consistency across blocks planted years apart.** Different crews,
different conditions, different levels of establishment care. It shows up at
harvest as variable maturity and variable yield within a nominally uniform
planting.

**The forecast-versus-result gap.** Publicly stated harvest forecasts and reported
outcomes for 2021 differ substantially. That is a normal feature of a young
orchard, and it is a good argument for not publishing forecasts as though they were
results — a discipline this site applies retrospectively.
