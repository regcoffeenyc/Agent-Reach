## The site

Udabno lies in the Sagarejo district of Kakheti, in eastern Georgia. The name
means "desert" in Georgian, which is a fair description of the starting condition:
low rainfall, high summer temperatures, sparse vegetation cover, erodible soils,
and no history of intensive commercial horticulture.

Land like this is available and cheap for a reason. Every input a horticultural
operation depends on — water, power, roads, workable soil — either did not exist
or existed inadequately. The development problem was therefore not "how do we grow
almonds here" but "what has to be built before growing almonds here is possible".

## What the work involved

### Soil assessment

The first task was establishing what the soil actually was, block by block, rather
than what regional survey data suggested. That means pits — dug, described,
photographed, and interpreted.

The recurring finding on land of this type is a dense subsoil layer that roots will
not enter. It may be geological, or the result of long-term consolidation under
extensive grazing. Either way it sets a ceiling on rooting volume, and in a
semi-arid climate a shallow rooting volume means the trees depend entirely on
irrigation frequency, with no buffer.

<div class="gm-firsthand">

The gap between the soil map and the pit was larger and more frequent than I
expected at the outset. Blocks that mapped as similar behaved differently; blocks
that mapped as poor were sometimes fine below 60 cm. We moved to treating survey
data as a sampling plan rather than a finding — it told us where to dig, not what
we would find.

That change cost time early and saved considerably more later, because it stopped
us specifying uniform treatment across ground that was not uniform.

</div>

### Subsoil preparation

Where assessment confirmed a restriction, it was opened mechanically before
planting. This is the least reversible decision in the whole project: once trees
are in, the subsoil is inaccessible for twenty-five years.

### Water

Nothing else was possible without this. The work covered source development,
storage to buffer supply variability against a strongly seasonal demand curve,
mainline and distribution construction, filtration, and fertigation capability.

Two design points proved decisive. First, sizing against **peak** demand during nut
fill rather than average annual demand — a system adequate on average fails at the
exact moment yield is set. Second, designing for distribution uniformity and then
actually measuring it in service, because uniformity degrades through emitter
blockage and pressure variation and the degradation is invisible from a vehicle.

See [Irrigation and Agricultural Infrastructure](/irrigation-infrastructure/).

### Erosion control

On erodible semi-arid soil, every operation that disturbs the surface creates
exposure. Alignment was the primary control: rip lines, rows and traffic lanes
across the slope rather than down it, because a rip line running downhill is a
drain and, on this soil, the beginning of a gully.

Beyond alignment: permanent inter-row cover where the water budget allowed,
treatment of the specific points where water concentrates — road edges, culvert
outlets, headlands, block corners — and an operational rule against cultivating
dry bare soil in wind.

See [Reducing Erosion in Semi-Arid Agricultural Land](/articles/reducing-erosion-semi-arid-land/).

### Infrastructure

Roads capable of carrying loaded harvest traffic in all weather; turning and
loading areas sized for harvest machinery; workshop, fuel and storage facilities;
staff facilities; and power.

*Investor.ge* records this in my own words at the time: "Before Udabno started
business in Kakheti there was no internet and television in the local village. We
built high voltage electrical lines." The same report notes support provided to
local schools and kindergartens.

That is worth stating plainly because it is easy to underestimate: an agricultural
project on undeveloped land ends up building public infrastructure, because the
project cannot function without it and nobody else is going to.

### Mechanization

Machinery capable of working heavy, dry, compacted ground — with the accompanying
problem that the mass required for traction in deep tillage causes compaction in
every other operation. Segregating heavy work from routine work is part of the
design rather than an afterthought.

See [Agricultural Mechanization](/mechanization/).

### Mixed production

The operation was not almonds alone. *Investor.ge* reports approximately 150,000
nursery rootstocks, livestock of 74 buffalo, 285 cows and 1,500 sheep, and
extensive berry, vegetable and vine production. A *Caucasus Business Week*
interview also refers to wheat, including Georgian endemic varieties.

Mixed production on a project like this is partly risk management — a perennial
crop takes years to bear, and annual crops and livestock generate revenue and
occupy the workforce meanwhile — and partly agronomic, since livestock integration
and rotation contribute to the soil work.

## Employment

Published figures differ. A *Caucasus Business Week* interview gives 150 permanent
and 200–500 seasonal employees; *Investor.ge* reports "over 300 people per season"
in the Sagarejo region. Both are reported here rather than averaged into a single
convenient number.

## What did not work first time

A case study without this section is marketing.

**Treating mapped soil as known soil.** Corrected early, but it cost time and
produced specifications for uniform treatment on non-uniform ground.

**Underestimating infrastructure lead times.** Roads, power and workshop capacity
were treated as supporting work while planting was the priority. They then became
the constraint on operations for longer than they should have. Infrastructure
deferred is infrastructure that limits you later, at higher cost.

**Sequencing planting against nursery supply rather than irrigation readiness.**
When trees arrive on the nursery's schedule and irrigation is on the
construction schedule, blocks get planted before they can be watered properly.
First-year establishment suffers, and the effect stays visible in the canopy for
years. The fix is to phase planting against infrastructure and accept holding or
losing nursery stock instead — a smaller cost, paid once.

**Assuming pilot-block performance would scale.** Pilot blocks get the best land,
the most attention and the most experienced people. Cost and quality figures
derived from them are optimistic for the full-scale operation.

## Outcome

An almond orchard of approximately 2,300 hectares developed from 2017, with
irrigation infrastructure, mechanization capability, processing capacity and
supporting infrastructure — on land that had not previously supported intensive
commercial horticulture.

During the period of my leadership, Udabno Group's "Udabno Regenerative
Transition" project was named the SME-category winner under SDG 12 at the 2023
Corporate Responsibility Award — Business for SDGs (UN Global Compact Network
Georgia, 12 October 2023). An **organisational** award to the project team.
