Goderdzi Metreveli is a Georgian agricultural executive and engineering
professional whose work has centred on the development of commercial agriculture
in the semi-arid Sagarejo district of Kakheti, in eastern Georgia.

His name is associated in independent publications with the Udabno agricultural
operation, part of Adjara Group. Reporting by *EastFruit* in September 2021 and
by *Investor.ge* in April 2022 identifies him as Director of Udabno. A technical
brief published in October 2021 by Chemonics under the USAID-funded Zrda
Activity quotes him as Managing Director of Adjara Group. A *Caucasus Business
Week* interview describes him as Director of Udabno Shelling, the operation's
processing arm.

## Working in a semi-arid environment

Udabno is a low-rainfall, erosion-prone landscape that had not previously
supported intensive commercial horticulture. That is not an incidental detail —
it determines the whole shape of the work. Land that has never been irrigated
has no water infrastructure. Land that has never been cropped intensively often
has a dense, poorly structured subsoil that will not admit roots. Land with thin
vegetation cover and summer wind loses topsoil the moment it is disturbed.

Converting it meant working on several fronts at once: assessing soil profiles
and identifying restrictive layers, opening those layers mechanically before
planting, establishing water supply and distribution where none existed,
building roads and operational buildings, and selecting machinery capable of
working heavy, dry ground without compacting it further.

My own focus within that work has been the decisions taken before planting.
Soil-profile assessment, subsoil preparation, row alignment, drainage and
irrigation layout are all things that constrain an orchard for its entire
productive life and are expensive or impossible to revisit once trees are in the
ground. Getting them wrong is not a setback that can be corrected in a later
season; it is a permanent reduction in what the block can do.

## Almond orchard development

*EastFruit* (2021) and *Investor.ge* (2022) report the Udabno almond orchard as
covering approximately 2,300 hectares, developed from 2017, with more than
675,000 trees of Spanish and Italian origin. *EastFruit* reports the varieties in
production as including Guara, Supernova and Lauren — Guara and Supernova
designated for European export — with fruit calibre in the 29–30 mm range.

At that scale the difficult problems are rarely horticultural. They are problems
of sequencing and matching: planting no faster than the irrigation system being
built alongside can serve; sizing harvest capacity against processing capacity
rather than against orchard area; and keeping establishment quality consistent
across blocks planted years apart by different crews under different conditions.

## Processing and the value chain

The operation developed both primary and secondary almond-processing capacity.
*EastFruit* reported in 2021 that new equipment would allow drying and shelling
to be done in-house, and that management intended to offer processing services
to other Georgian almond and walnut growers.

That last point addresses a structural problem in Georgian horticulture. Most
producers are too small to justify processing equipment of their own, so they
sell unprocessed and capture the lowest margin in the chain. Regional processing
capacity that independent growers can access changes what their crop is worth,
which is a different kind of outcome from expanding a single company's own
orchard.

## Standards and market access

I have contributed publicly to the discussion of agricultural standards in
Georgia. The Chemonics/USAID technical brief on the GeoGAP certification scheme
quotes my view of why a locally appropriate standard matters:

> "In general, we think that implementation of GeoGAP standards, and going
> through certification, will give farmers [the] ability to also sell their
> products at higher prices in the retail sector."
>
> <cite>Chemonics / USAID Zrda Activity, *A Local Certification System in Georgia*, October 2021</cite>

International certification schemes are export-oriented, expensive and demanding.
Most Georgian producers cannot reach them, and the ones who can sell to exporters
to recoup the cost. That leaves a gap: growers who want to demonstrate quality but
cannot afford an international scheme, and domestic buyers who would pay a
premium if there were anything to verify it. A locally calibrated standard is a
step onto that ladder rather than a substitute for the top of it.

## Regenerative transition

During the period of my leadership, Udabno Group's "Udabno Regenerative
Transition" project was named the SME-category winner under Sustainable
Development Goal 12 at the 2023 Corporate Responsibility Award — Business for
SDGs, announced by UN Global Compact Network Georgia on 12 October 2023.

This was an award to the organisation and its project team. It is recorded here
because the project is relevant to the work described on this site, not as a
personal distinction.

## How I work

The projects described on this site were delivered by teams — agronomists,
engineers, machine operators, contractors, consultants and partner
organisations. What is described here is my role within that shared work.

The habit that has served best is measuring rather than assuming. A soil map is
a hypothesis; a pit dug in the block is evidence, and the two disagree more often
than people expect. A machinery configuration that works in one soil type may
smear and seal in another 400 metres away. Testing on one block before committing
a fleet costs a few days and has repeatedly saved seasons.
