Mechanization decisions on a large operation are usually discussed as procurement.
They are better understood as capability decisions: the machinery available
determines what soil preparation is physically possible, which determines what the
site can become.

On heavy, dry, compacted ground this is not a marginal effect. If the available
traction cannot pull a ripper through a restrictive layer at the required depth,
that layer stays, and the orchard's rooting volume is permanently limited — not by
agronomy, but by the equipment on hand when the decision was made.

## Selection principles

**Match the machine to the soil, not to the brochure.** Manufacturer performance
figures are established under favourable conditions. Draught requirements in dry,
hard, stony ground are substantially higher than in the conditions used for
specification, and progress rates correspondingly lower.

**Power is necessary but not sufficient.** Deep tillage needs drawbar pull, which
requires weight, appropriate ballast, tyre or track configuration and adequate
traction. Engine power that cannot be transmitted to the ground is not useful.
This is why tracked or heavily ballasted machines earn their cost in deep-ripping
work while a nominally similar wheeled tractor cannot do the job.

**Weight is a liability outside the job it is needed for.** The same mass that
provides traction for ripping causes compaction in every other operation. A fleet
built entirely around the heaviest task compacts the soil it was bought to
improve. Segregating heavy work from routine work — by machine, by traffic lane,
and by timing — is part of the design.

**Controlled traffic where practical.** Confining compaction to permanent lanes
protects the rest of the block. In orchards this is largely automatic once rows are
established, which makes the alignment decisions at establishment more
consequential than they appear.

**Serviceability in the actual location.** A machine that cannot be repaired
locally within the harvest window is a risk regardless of its specification. Parts
availability, dealer support and the skill base to maintain it matter as much as
its capability. In a market without an established machinery base, this frequently
outranks performance.

## Implement matching

The implement, not the tractor, does the work, and matching it to soil condition
is where results are won or lost.

- **Ripper configuration** — shank number, spacing, geometry and wear parts,
  matched to the depth and the soil. See [Deep-Ripping and Soil Preparation](/deep-ripping/).
- **Wear parts as a consumable.** In stony ground, wear is a major operating cost
  and worn points change the geometry of the work: a blunt shank smears where a
  sharp one fractures. Wear affects results, not just cost.
- **Depth control.** Consistency matters more than maximum depth. Variable depth
  leaves untreated columns.
- **Matching to moisture.** The same implement in the same soil produces fracture
  or smearing depending on moisture. See [Correct Soil Moisture for Deep Ripping](/articles/soil-moisture-for-deep-ripping/).

## Harvest machinery

Mechanical almond harvest is a chain — shake, sweep, pick up, transport, receive —
and it runs at the speed of its slowest element.

- **Trunk or canopy shakers** removing the crop
- **Sweepers** windrowing from the orchard floor
- **Pick-up machines** collecting the windrow
- **Transport** to the processing facility
- **Receiving and drying** capacity

The failure mode is capacity mismatch. An additional shaker adds nothing if the
pick-up machinery or the receiving facility is already the constraint — it just
moves the queue. Fleet sizing has to be calculated backward from processing
throughput, not forward from orchard area.

**Orchard floor condition determines harvest efficiency and product cleanliness.**
It is a decision made at establishment: levelling, stone management, surface
shaping. An uneven floor costs harvest efficiency and product quality every season
for the orchard's life, and cannot be meaningfully improved once trees are in.

## Maintenance capability

On a large operation, maintenance capability is part of the machinery decision.

- **Workshop capacity** proportionate to the fleet
- **Parts inventory** for critical failures during harvest, when a delay is not
  recoverable within the season
- **Operator training** — the same machine in different hands produces measurably
  different work, and in deep tillage the difference is large
- **Scheduled maintenance timed against the operational calendar**, so it happens
  before the window rather than during it
- **Records** to inform replacement decisions with data rather than impression

<div class="gm-firsthand">

Operator skill is consistently underweighted in mechanization planning. In deep
ripping the operator controls depth consistency, speed, and the judgement about
whether conditions are right at all — and that judgement is the one that decides
whether the pass fractures the soil or smears it. Training operators to read the
soil behind the machine, and giving them the authority to stop when it looks
wrong, produced better outcomes than any equipment upgrade.
