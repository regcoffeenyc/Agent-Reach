An almond orchard of a few hectares is a horticultural project. An almond orchard
of a few thousand hectares is a logistics and infrastructure project that happens
to grow almonds. The agronomy is largely known; what determines the outcome is
whether planting, irrigation construction, harvest capacity and processing
capacity stay in step with one another.

*EastFruit* (2021) and *Investor.ge* (2022) report the Udabno orchard at
approximately 2,300 hectares, developed from 2017, with more than 675,000 trees
of Spanish and Italian origin, and varieties including Guara, Supernova and Lauren.

## Site assessment before commitment

The questions that decide whether a site should be planted at all, in order:

1. **Is there secure water, in sufficient quantity, of adequate quality?** Almonds
   are a high-water-requirement crop with no dormant tolerance for drought during
   nut fill. A site without secure water is not an almond site, whatever else is
   in its favour. Water quality matters as much as quantity — salinity limits are
   real and almonds are relatively sensitive.
2. **Is the soil deep enough, and can it be made deep enough?** See below.
3. **Does the site drain?** Almonds are intolerant of waterlogging and
   susceptible to root and crown diseases in poorly drained ground.
4. **What is the frost risk at bloom?** Almonds flower very early. In continental
   climates this is the dominant yield risk. Cold-air drainage and site
   topography matter more than mean temperature statistics.
5. **Is the ground workable by the machinery the crop requires?** Mechanical
   harvesting needs a firm, level, well-shaped orchard floor. Slope, stoniness
   and surface irregularity are harvest constraints, not just establishment ones.

A "no" to question 1 or 4 should stop the project. In practice these are the
questions most often answered optimistically.

## Soil requirements and preparation

Almonds want deep, well-drained soil with no restriction in the rooting zone.
They are unusually unforgiving of two conditions: waterlogging, and a shallow
restrictive layer in a climate where the tree depends on stored soil water
between irrigations.

Which means: **the soil work happens before planting, or it does not happen.**
Profile assessment by pit, identification of restrictive layers, deep ripping
where justified, drainage where required, then levelling and irrigation.

See [Deep-Ripping and Soil Preparation](/deep-ripping/) and

## Variety selection in continental and semi-arid conditions

*EastFruit* reports Guara, Supernova and Lauren among the varieties in production
at Udabno, with Guara and Supernova designated for European export and fruit
calibre of 29–30 mm.

The selection criteria that matter in a continental climate:

- **Bloom timing.** Later bloom reduces frost exposure. In a climate with spring
  frost risk this is the single most important varietal characteristic, ahead of
  yield potential.
- **Self-fertility.** Self-fertile varieties such as Guara and Supernova remove
  dependence on pollinator layout and on bee activity during a narrow, possibly
  cold, bloom window. At scale this substantially de-risks the crop.
- **Kernel characteristics for the target market.** European buyers specify
  calibre, shape, colour and shell characteristics. Variety choice is a market
  decision as much as an agronomic one, and it is made once for twenty-five years.
- **Harvest timing spread.** Multiple varieties with staggered maturity spread the
  load on harvest machinery and processing capacity. Planting a single variety
  across a large area concentrates the entire harvest into one narrow window,
  which is a capacity problem disguised as a simplification.
- **Disease susceptibility** under local humidity and rainfall patterns.

## Block layout and planting sequence

Layout decisions are permanent, and they are made when the least is known about
the site.

- **Row orientation** balances light interception, machinery efficiency, and — on
  slopes — erosion control. Where these conflict on erodible ground, erosion
  control wins.
- **Block size** trades machinery efficiency against management granularity. Very
  large blocks are efficient to work and difficult to manage differentially when
  soil or water varies across them.
- **Headlands and turning space** sized for the largest machine that will ever
  work the block, including harvest equipment. Retrofitting turning space is
  expensive.
- **Road layout** determining harvest logistics for the orchard's life.
- **Planting phased against infrastructure**, not against nursery availability.

That last point is the one that causes the most trouble. Nursery trees arrive on a
schedule set by the nursery. Irrigation gets built on a schedule set by
construction. When planting follows the trees rather than the water, blocks go in
before they can be irrigated properly, and first-year establishment suffers in a
way that shows in the canopy for years.

## Irrigation

Almonds in semi-arid conditions are entirely irrigation-dependent. Design
determines yield ceiling.

Key decisions: system type and emitter layout; capacity sized to peak demand, not
average; distribution uniformity as a design and maintenance target; filtration
matched to water source; fertigation capability; and monitoring at more than one
depth.

The design error with the longest consequences is sizing to average rather than
peak demand. A system that cannot meet peak demand during nut fill produces a
water deficit at the moment yield is set — every year, permanently.

See [Irrigation Planning for Almond Orchards](/articles/irrigation-planning-almond-orchards/)

## Harvest planning and machinery

Mechanical almond harvest is a chain: shake, sweep, pick up, transport, then
receive and process. The chain runs at the speed of its slowest element, and the
window is short.

Planning implications:

- Capacity is calculated backward from **processing throughput**, not forward from
  orchard area
- Harvest window length is set by variety maturity spread and weather risk
- Transport and receiving capacity must match field output, or the field stops
- Orchard floor condition determines harvest efficiency and product cleanliness —
  a decision made at establishment
- Machinery must be maintained to work continuously through the window; a
  breakdown during harvest is not recoverable within the season

See [Machinery Requirements for Large Almond Operations](/articles/machinery-large-almond-operations/)
and [Agricultural Mechanization](/mechanization/).

## Orchard-to-processing planning

The mismatch that catches large operations is between orchard growth and
processing capacity. Orchard area expands in one step — a planting decision.
Processing capacity expands in another — a capital project with a long lead time.
If the orchard reaches full bearing before processing capacity is in place, the
crop is sold unprocessed at the lowest margin in the chain, which is the outcome
the orchard investment was meant to avoid.

Planning processing capacity against the orchard's **future** bearing area rather
than its current one is what avoids that.

## Preparing for European markets

*EastFruit* reports that Guara and Supernova plantings were designated for
European export. Meeting EU requirements is a systems question, not a
final-inspection question: maximum residue levels and therefore spray programme
discipline, aflatoxin control through drying and storage management, traceability
from block to consignment, hygiene standards in processing, and documentation
sufficient to survive an audit.

Most of these are decided in the orchard and in the processing facility's design,
not at the point of shipment.
