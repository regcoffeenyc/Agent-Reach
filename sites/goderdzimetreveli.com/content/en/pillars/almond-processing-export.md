An orchard grows a crop. A value chain decides what that crop is worth. In
Georgian horticulture the gap between the two has been the structural problem:
producers grow well and sell unprocessed, capturing the thinnest margin in the
chain while processing value is captured elsewhere.

Building processing capacity alongside the orchard was an attempt to close that
gap — for the operation itself, and, as reported by *EastFruit* in 2021, with the
stated intention of offering processing services to other Georgian almond and
walnut growers.

## Why processing capacity governs the orchard

Processing capacity is not a downstream detail. It sets the harvest calendar.

Almonds are harvested in a short window and must be dried to a safe moisture
content quickly. If receiving and drying capacity is below field output, the field
stops — or product waits in conditions where quality degrades and aflatoxin risk
rises. Neither is acceptable, and the second is a food-safety failure rather than
an efficiency loss.

So the planning direction runs backward: processing throughput determines harvest
rate, which determines machinery and transport requirements, which determines how
much area can be brought to bearing in a given year.

Getting this wrong in the other direction — orchard reaching full bearing before
processing is commissioned — forces the crop out unprocessed at the lowest
available margin, which defeats the purpose of the orchard investment.

## Primary processing

**Hulling and shelling.** Removing hull and shell from the harvested nut. Throughput
here is what usually defines "processing capacity" and is the constraint on
receiving rate.

**Drying.** The critical food-safety step. Almonds must reach and hold a moisture
content low enough to prevent mould growth and aflatoxin development. Drying
capacity, not shelling capacity, is often the real bottleneck, and it is the one
that cannot be compromised.

**Cleaning and sizing.** Removing foreign material, grading by calibre. European
buyers specify calibre, so this determines what can be sold into which
specification.

**Sorting.** Removing defective kernels, shell fragments and foreign material.
Increasingly optical, and directly tied to achieving export grade.

**Storage.** Controlled conditions to hold quality between harvest and shipment.
Storage capacity is what converts a harvest-time surplus into year-round sales
rather than a forced sale at harvest prices.

## Secondary processing

Secondary processing converts kernel into products with longer shelf life, higher
value and different market channels — flour, oil, and milk among them.

The strategic argument for it: it absorbs product that does not meet whole-kernel
export specification. Every orchard produces a proportion of broken, undersized or
cosmetically imperfect kernel that is perfectly good food but cannot be sold as
premium whole kernel. Without secondary processing that product is sold at a
discount. With it, the discount becomes an input cost to a higher-value product.

That changes the economics of the whole operation, and it reduces the pressure to
achieve unrealistic export-grade percentages.

## Quality standards for European markets

EU market access is a systems requirement, not an inspection at the border.

- **Maximum residue levels.** Determined by the orchard spray programme, years
  before shipment. A single non-compliant application can disqualify a
  consignment.
- **Aflatoxin limits.** EU limits for tree nuts are strict. Control is through
  harvest timing, rapid drying, storage conditions and sorting — all process
  design, not testing.
- **Traceability.** Block to consignment, documented and auditable.
- **Hygiene and HACCP** in processing facility design and operation.
- **Documentation** sufficient to survive an audit rather than merely to accompany
  a shipment.

The pattern: almost every EU requirement is met by decisions made upstream in the
orchard and in the design of the facility. Very little can be fixed at the point
of export.

## GeoGAP and commercial market access

International certification schemes such as GLOBALG.A.P. are export-oriented,
expensive and demanding. Most Georgian producers cannot reach them, and those who
can sell to exporters to recoup the cost. That leaves a gap on both sides: growers
who want to demonstrate quality but cannot afford an international scheme, and
domestic buyers willing to pay for quality with no way to verify it.

GeoGAP was created by the Georgian Farmers' Association with USAID and Chemonics
support as a scaled-down, locally calibrated stepping-stone toward the
international schemes. I am quoted in the Chemonics/USAID technical brief on why
that matters:

> "In general, we think that implementation of GeoGAP standards, and going through
> certification, will give farmers [the] ability to also sell their products at
> higher prices in the retail sector."
>
> <cite>Chemonics / USAID Zrda Activity, <em>A Local Certification System in Georgia</em>, October 2021</cite>

The same brief records that Adjara Group planned to implement good agricultural
practices and earn GeoGAP certification at its almond orchard, berry farm and
vineyard. That is documented as a plan; this site does not claim the certification
was achieved.

## Regional processing for independent growers

*EastFruit* reported in 2021 that management intended to offer processing services
to other Georgian almond and walnut growers, with a view to a regional processing
base.

This addresses the structural problem directly. An individual grower with a few
hectares cannot justify hulling, drying and sorting equipment. Without access to
it, the crop is sold unprocessed at the lowest margin — so the grower captures
little, has no incentive to invest in quality, and the regional crop stays low
value in aggregate.

Accessible regional processing changes the calculation: quality becomes worth
achieving because it becomes sellable. That is a different kind of outcome from
expanding one company's own orchard, and a more durable one.
