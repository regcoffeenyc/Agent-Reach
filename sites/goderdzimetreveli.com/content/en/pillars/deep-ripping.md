Deep ripping is the most consequential soil operation in orchard establishment
and the least reversible. Done correctly on a site that needs it, it determines
the rooting volume available to a tree for the next twenty-five years. Done on the
wrong site, at the wrong moisture, or in the wrong direction, it wastes
substantial money and can leave the soil worse than before.

This page covers when ripping is justified, how to decide the parameters, and —
the section most often missing from equipment-vendor literature — when not to do
it at all.

## Why the subsoil decides an orchard's ceiling

An almond tree's productive capacity is bounded by the volume of soil its roots
can occupy and extract water from. Above ground, most limitations can be managed
season by season: nutrition, pests, canopy, irrigation scheduling. Below ground,
almost nothing can be changed after planting.

If a dense layer sits at 40 cm, the tree has 40 cm of soil regardless of what
the profile looks like below it. In a semi-arid environment that constraint
compounds: a shallow rooting volume holds less water, so the tree depends entirely
on irrigation frequency, which means any interruption in supply becomes a stress
event rather than an inconvenience.

Deep ripping exists to remove that constraint before the trees are planted. That
is its whole purpose, and it is the test for whether it is needed: **is there a
layer restricting root development, and will fracturing it produce a rooting
volume the crop can use?**

If the answer to either half is no, ripping is not indicated.

## Subsoiling versus deep ripping

The terms are used interchangeably in practice, and the distinction matters
because the operations have different purposes and cost profiles.

| | Subsoiling | Deep ripping |
|---|---|---|
| Typical depth | Below the cultivation layer, moderate | Substantially deeper |
| Purpose | Break a tillage-induced pan | Fracture a deeper natural or structural restriction |
| Frequency | May be repeated in a rotation | Once, before establishment |
| Power requirement | Moderate | High to very high |
| Context | Arable and annual cropping | Perennial establishment, orchards, vineyards |

The practical difference: subsoiling addresses a problem created by farming, and
recurs because the cause recurs. Deep ripping addresses a deeper restriction —
often geological or from long-term consolidation — and is a one-off opportunity
taken before a perennial crop goes in, because afterwards the ground is
inaccessible.

## Identifying a restrictive layer

The mistake that costs the most money is ripping a site without confirming a
restriction exists. The mistake that costs the most yield is not ripping a site
that has one.

Both are avoided the same way: **dig a pit and look.**

Soil maps and survey data indicate what to expect; they do not establish what is
present in a particular block. In my experience the two disagree often enough
that a map should be treated as a hypothesis to test rather than a finding.

What to look for in the profile face:

- **An abrupt change in density** you can feel with a knife or probe
- **Platy or massive structure** where the layer above and below is crumb or blocky
- **Roots turning sideways** at a consistent depth — the most direct evidence
  available, and it costs nothing to observe
- **Colour or mottling change** indicating water perching on the layer
- **Standing water** in the pit at the layer boundary after rain

Supporting measurements — a penetrometer traverse, bulk-density sampling — refine
the picture. They do not replace opening the ground.

## Soil moisture is the decision that determines success

If one thing on this page is worth remembering: **soil moisture at the time of
ripping matters more than depth, spacing, machine power or operator skill.**

Soil fails in two modes. Dry, brittle soil **fractures** — cracks propagate
outward and upward from the shank, and the result is a network of fissures roots
can exploit. Wet, plastic soil **deforms** — it flows around the shank and closes
behind it, and the tine polishes a smeared surface that is denser and less
permeable than the layer being treated.

The second outcome is worse than not ripping. Money spent, and a new restriction
created.

This is why ripping is done at the dry end of the season, when the profile is dry
through the target depth — not when the calendar or the contractor's schedule
suits. A site that is not dry enough should wait, even at the cost of a season.

## Depth, spacing and direction

**Depth.** Deep enough to penetrate and fracture through the restriction, and no
deeper. Depth beyond the restriction consumes fuel and traction for nothing —
draught rises steeply and non-linearly with depth — and can bring unsuitable
material upward. The pit tells you the depth; the machine does not.

**Spacing.** Close enough that fracture zones from adjacent passes intersect,
leaving no untreated columns between them. Fracture spreads further in dry,
brittle soil than in moist soil, so spacing and moisture are a single coupled
decision.

**Direction.** Two considerations, sometimes in tension. Ripping lines should
relate sensibly to the future orchard rows, so that tree positions sit in treated
ground rather than between passes. And ripping lines must not become preferential
water paths down a slope — a rip line running downhill on sloping ground is a
drain, and on erodible semi-arid soil it is the beginning of a gully. On slopes,
ripping across the slope; where that conflicts with row orientation, the erosion
risk takes precedence.

## Sequence with drainage and irrigation

Order of operations, and the reasoning:

1. **Assessment** — pits, profiles, drainage evaluation
2. **Drainage** — if the site needs it, install before ripping. Ripping into a
   site with an unresolved water table produces fractures that fill with water
3. **Deep ripping** — at correct moisture, correct depth and direction
4. **Surface preparation and levelling** — after the ground has settled
5. **Irrigation installation** — after ripping, never before
6. **Planting**

Step 5 is where expensive mistakes happen. Ripping after irrigation is installed
means either destroying the system or leaving untreated strips exactly where the
trees will be planted. The sequence is not flexible.

The relationship with drainage runs both ways: ripping without drainage on a site
that needs both creates a network of fissures that concentrates water with nowhere
to go, which is worse than either problem alone.

## Post-ripping settlement

Ripped ground is loosened, and it will settle — through its own weight, through
wetting and drying cycles, and through the first significant rainfall.

Practical consequences:

- **Do not plant into freshly ripped, unsettled ground.** Trees planted before
  settlement end up too deep as the soil drops around them, and stem burial at
  the graft union causes losses that appear years later.
- Allow a wetting cycle and a settlement period between ripping and planting.
  Natural rainfall is preferable; irrigation can be used to accelerate it.
- Re-level after settlement. Surface irregularity that appears after settling
  affects machinery access and surface water movement for the orchard's life.
- Expect and check for surface subsidence along rip lines.

## When deep ripping should not be done

The section most often omitted. Ripping is contraindicated when:

- **There is no restrictive layer.** Loosening already permeable soil achieves
  nothing and reduces its bearing capacity.
- **Soil is too wet.** Smearing creates a worse restriction than the original.
- **The restriction is too deep** to reach with available machinery — partial
  penetration that stops within a dense layer can worsen conditions locally.
- **The layer is a genuine barrier serving a purpose**, such as a horizon
  perching water above saline subsoil. Fracturing it can bring salts into the
  root zone. On saline or sodic sites, ripping without a salinity assessment is a
  serious risk.
- **Subsoil material is unsuitable** — strongly sodic, saline, or acidic
  material brought upward can be worse than the restriction.
- **Slope and erodibility make it unsafe** without erosion control in place.
- **The economics do not work.** Ripping is expensive in fuel, machine hours and
  wear. On a marginal site the same capital may do more for water infrastructure
  or drainage.

## Cost and operational planning

Deep ripping is among the most expensive single field operations in orchard
establishment. Cost drivers: depth (the dominant factor — draught rises sharply
with it), soil condition and moisture, spacing, and machine and implement wear,
which is substantial in stony ground.

Planning points:

- Cost is per hectare **at a given depth and spacing**. Quotes that omit either
  are not comparable.
- Progress rates in dry, hard ground are slower than machinery specifications
  suggest.
- The narrow window when soil is dry enough is also when contractors are busiest.
  Book early or own the capability.
- Budget for wear parts, not just hours.
- Include the settlement period in the establishment timetable. It is not slack.

## Quality control

Verify the work rather than assuming it. Dig a pit **across** the rip line after
the operation and look for: actual depth achieved against specification, fracture
extending laterally toward the adjacent pass, no smeared or polished surfaces, and
no untreated column between passes.

Checking a small number of pits across a block, while the machine is still on
site, is the difference between a specification and an outcome.
